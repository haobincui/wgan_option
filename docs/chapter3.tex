\chapter{Volatility Surface Prediction with Textual Information}\label{ch: chapter: vol_surface}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:ch3: introduction}

Modeling the Implied Volatility Surface (IVS) has long been a central challenge in financial economics and engineering, as volatility plays a crucial role in option pricing, risk management, and derivative valuation. 


Early approaches, such as the polynomial parameterization proposed by \cite{dumas1998implied} and spline-based interpolations (\cite{kahale2004arbitrage}; \cite{wang2004no}), sought to represent the volatility smile and term structure across maturities. Stochastic volatility frameworks, including the Heston Model (\cite{Heston:1993}) and the Stochastic Alpha Beta Rho (SABR) model (\cite{sabr2002}), are typically calibrated to the observed volatility structure of the market.

Recent advances in machine learning (ML) have enabled the use of non-parametric and deep learning architectures that can learn complex patterns directly from data. For example, \cite{ackerer2020deep} applied deep neural networks to estimate IVS non-parametrically, \cite{medvedev2022multistep} employed Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks to capture temporal dependencies, and \cite{kelly2023deep} utilized Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to model spatial patterns across moneyness and maturity dimensions. These traditional IVS models rely primarily on historical and quantitative data. 



On the other hand, a growing body of research demonstrates that text-derived information significantly influences financial markets. Textual information offers a second description of the state facing market participants. Corporate disclosures, analyst reports, central bank statements, and financial news releases describe expectations and uncertainty in language. For instance, \cite{manela2017news} introduced the News Volatility Index to link media sentiment to equity market volatility, while \cite{bybee2024business} found that business news can forecast macroeconomic indicators. Similarly, \cite{vergote2012} showed that central bank communications affect the implied volatility of rate options. Other studies confirm that financial sentiment extracted from textual data can predict returns and volatility (\cite{schumaker2009quantitative}; \cite{hagenau2012automated}; \cite{ke2019predicting}; \cite{li2020incorporating}).




The predictive usefulness of textual data depends partly on how the text is represented. Traditional approaches, including bag-of-words (BoW) models and dictionary-based sentiment measures, reduce documents to word frequencies or predefined sentiment categories. These representations are transparent and computationally efficient, but they have limited ability to capture word order, context, and semantic relationships. The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) marks a transformative breakthrough in this regard. LLMs encode linguistic information into high-dimensional embeddings that capture semantics, tone, and contextual dependencies. By converting text into numerical representations, these models bridge the gap between qualitative and quantitative information. Empirical studies (\cite{chen2021stock}; \cite{ji2021stock}; \cite{chen2022expected}) show that embeddings generated by Transformer-based architectures, such as BERT and GPT, can enhance predictive accuracy when combined with market variables. This integration of textual and numerical data enables richer modeling of how qualitative narratives and sentiment shifts influence asset prices and volatility.





These findings motivate a conditional forecasting exercise:  
Whether text embeddings improve short-horizon IVS forecasts?  The current IVS is itself derived from market prices and may already reflect publicly available news, investor expectations, and policy information. From an efficient-markets perspective, text that is already incorporated into option prices should provide little incremental predictive value after the current surface is observed \citep{fama1970}. Conversely, textual embeddings may retain information about the interpretation, tone, or expected consequences of news that is not fully captured by the contemporaneous IVS. The relevant forecasting question is therefore not simply whether news affects volatility, but whether its textual representation improves predictions of subsequent IVS changes conditional on the current market state.




This chapter addresses this question using an ML framework that combines the current IVS with LLM-derived text embeddings to forecast the surface over the next five-minute interval. A convolutional architecture models the spatial dependence of implied volatility across moneyness and maturity, while Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) incorporates the textual representation into intermediate network features. The empirical design compares the text-conditioned model with a surface-only benchmark, evaluates alternative text representations, and uses zero-text, shuffled-text, and inference-time replacement controls to examine whether any forecasting improvement can be attributed to correctly matched textual information.



This chapter is organized as follows. Section~\ref{ch3:sec:contribution} presents the research questions and contributions. Section~\ref{ch3:sec:lr} reviews the literature on IVS forecasting, machine learning, and textual information in financial markets. Section~\ref{ch3:sec:model} describes the ML forecasting framework and the incorporation of text embeddings. Section~\ref{ch3:sec:data} explains the construction of the market and textual datasets, and Section~\ref{sec: ch3: result} presents the empirical results.

\section{Research Question and Contribution}\label{ch3:sec:contribution}

The central research question is whether text helps predict the next stage of option-market repricing once the current market state is known. We operationalise this question through the subsequent IVS change. Let \(\mathcal I_t^{M}\) denote the model's observed market inputs, consisting of the current IVS and its current-side support and grid information. Let \(\mathbf w_t\) represent the articles assigned to that market transition and available by \(t\) under the recorded publication-time convention. The two conditioning sets and the forecast change are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:forecast_information_sets}
\mathcal{I}_t^{M+N}
=
\left(\mathcal{I}_t^{M},\mathbf{w}_t\right),
\qquad
\Delta_h\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t
=
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t+h}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t,
\qquad
h=5\text{ minutes}.
\end{equation}

Here, \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t\) summarizes the IVS observed over the preceding five-minute window, while \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t+h}\) summarizes the IVS over the subsequent five-minute window. The empirical null hypothesis is that adding the matched text representation \(\mathbf{w}_t\) produces no stable reduction in forecast loss beyond that achieved using the observed market inputs alone. This null hypothesis allows IVS changes to remain predictable from market information. Four research questions examine this financial motivation:



\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1: Model Effectiveness.} Given the current IVS, does the complete FiLM-CNN model improve prediction of the next five-minute surface change relative to a pure-CNN system trained without textual conditioning?

    \item \textbf{RQ2: Text Representation Effectiveness.} Conditional on the current IVS, how do LP embeddings, bag-of-words features, and sentiment scores compare in forecasting the next five-minute surface change under a common FiLM-CNN architecture and training process?

    \item \textbf{RQ3: Incremental Text Value.} Does correctly matched LP text improve forecasting accuracy relative to zero-text and shuffled-text controls?

    \item \textbf{RQ4: Greater Text Value during Events (Exploratory).} Is the incremental predictive value of correctly matched text greater during scheduled-announcement or market-jump periods than during comparable ordinary-market periods?
\end{itemize}



This study addresses these questions by adding textual embeddings to a forecasting system that already observes the current IVS. RQ1 compares complete forecasting systems, RQ2 compares text representations within the chosen architecture, and RQ3 tests incremental text value against equal-budget controls. RQ4 asks the directional question of whether that conditional value is greater during event periods than during comparable ordinary periods. 


The five-minute horizon focuses on the next observable adjustment in option-implied volatility. When a current-surface window already contains post-publication trading, its prices may reflect part of the news response. Such an observation tests whether the text remains useful after that response is included in the current state. 


Finally, the study contributes to empirical implied volatility forecasting in three ways:


\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A Conditional Forecasting Framework:} We integrate news information with current and future IVS observations using a FiLM-CNN framework. The model integrates text embeddings with the current IVS to forecast its subsequent evolution, which enables a direct assessment of the incremental predictive value of textual information.



\item \textbf{Identification of Textual Forecasting Gains:} We combine model- and representation-level comparisons with common-parent, equal-budget, and same-checkpoint controls. This design separates gains arising from model architecture and additional training from those attributable to correctly matched textual information.


\item \textbf{Evidence on the Limits of Incremental Text Value:} We find no statistically supported forecasting improvement from matched LP embeddings relative to the equal-budget zero-text control, while removing or replacing text at inference does not reduce forecast accuracy. These results show that representation rankings or an advantage over shuffled text alone do not establish stable incremental text value beyond the current IVS.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}\label{ch3:sec:lr}

The implied volatility surface (IVS), derived from market option prices, is a critical tool for traders, who monitor it closely to provide quotes, manage risk, and value their portfolios. Consequently, there is a growing body of literature devoted to modeling and forecasting the implied volatility surface.

% traditional models

Traditional approaches to modeling the surface can be broadly categorized into parametric and non-parametric models.

Parametric models aim to characterize the IVS using interpretable parameters. A seminal example is the polynomial parametrization studied by Dumas et al. (\cite{dumas1998implied}), which allows implied volatility to vary across strikes and maturities. Their analysis of deterministic volatility functions distinguishes a good contemporaneous fit from successful out-of-sample prediction and hedging; it does not assume that the observed implied-volatility surface is constant. Stochastic volatility models provide a different approach. Notable examples include the Heston Model (\cite{Heston:1993}) and the Stochastic Alpha Beta Rho (SABR) Model (\cite{sabr2002}), both of which model the underlying asset price and its volatility as stochastic processes. These models can represent volatility smiles and skews. In addition, econometric models such as the GARCH model (\cite{Bollerslev:1986}) capture conditional-volatility dynamics, including persistence and mean reversion.


In contrast, non-parametric models focus on fitting the IVS directly using flexible mathematical techniques without relying on interpretable parameters. Common methods include linear interpolation and cubic splines (\cite{kahale2004arbitrage}; \cite{wang2004no}), which are designed to produce smooth surfaces consistent with observed option prices. While non-parametric models offer greater flexibility and accuracy in fitting, they lack explanatory power—their parameters serve purely as curve-fitting tools without economic or probabilistic interpretation.



% ML models


Furthermore, with the rapid advancement of machine learning (ML) techniques across various domains, researchers have increasingly applied these methods to model the Implied Volatility Surface (IVS). Compared with traditional econometric and parametric models, ML-based approaches offer a notable advantage in handling high-dimensional datasets and capturing complex nonlinear relationships.


Early contributions, such as \cite{malliaris1996using} and \cite{guidolin2003option}, explored the use of neural networks and support vector machines to improve IVS prediction. More recent research has benefited from significant improvements in computational power, enabling the use of more complex neural architectures to capture the nuanced dynamics of the volatility surface. For example, \cite{ackerer2020deep} employed deep neural networks for non-parametric IVS modeling, \cite{medvedev2022multistep} utilized long short-term memory (LSTM) networks to incorporate temporal dependencies, and \cite{kelly2023deep} adopted convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to extract spatial patterns across moneyness and maturity dimensions.

In addition, the application of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) has recently emerged as a promising approach in financial risk management. For example, \cite{ge2025gan} shows the GAN model is outperformaned than other parametric models. \cite{vuletic2024volgan} further demonstrated that GAN-based models can incorporate the arbitrage-free conditions when generating the implied volatility surface. Moreover, \cite{cont2025tail} showed that GANs achieve remarkable performance in managing tail risk.



Much of this modelling literature uses numerical market inputs, including option and underlying prices. News studies provide a related motivation: for example, \cite{vergote2012} examines how European Central Bank announcements affect the implied volatility of 3M EURIBOR rate options. Such evidence concerns the market response to information. For forecasting, a further question is whether the text contains useful information about subsequent changes after the response already present in the current IVS has been conditioned upon. The present study addresses that conditional prediction question.



% incorporate text data

On the other hand, in recent years, there has been growing interest in incorporating textual information into predictive models, particularly in the domain of stock price forecasting. Several studies have demonstrated the significant impact of news on financial markets. For instance, \cite{manela2017news} developed the News Volatility Index to assess its influence on the equity market, while \cite{bybee2024business} provided evidence that business news can forecast macroeconomic indicators. In addition, a substantial body of literature has explored the extraction of sentiment signals from financial news and their predictive effects on market movements. Notable examples include \cite{schumaker2009quantitative}, \cite{hagenau2012automated}, \cite{ke2019predicting}, and \cite{li2020incorporating}, which investigate how sentiment derived from news articles can serve as a critical explanatory variable. Earlier research, such as \cite{schumaker2009textual} and \cite{de2014evaluating}, employed text-mining techniques—most notably, the bag-of-words approach—to quantify unstructured textual data for use in econometric models.


More recently, the emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) has significantly improved the integration of textual data into prediction frameworks. LLMs are capable of encoding complex semantic information into high-dimensional embedding vectors, enabling more nuanced representation of textual inputs. Studies such as \cite{chen2021stock}, \cite{ji2021stock}, and \cite{chen2022expected} utilize these embeddings to transform unstructured financial news into structured numerical inputs. In particular, embeddings generated by BERT-style encoders and GPT-style decoders have been shown to enhance forecasting performance when used alongside traditional quantitative indicators.



This literature motivates testing embeddings as an additional forecasting input, but does not establish their incremental value for the next five-minute IVS change. That value depends on what the current surface already reveals, when the text becomes available relative to the forecast origin, and how the fitted model uses it. These considerations link the literature to the information-set comparison in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch3:forecast_information_sets}.



\section{Model Details}\label{ch3:sec:model}

Inspired by \cite{vuletic2024volgan}, who apply a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN; \citealp{goodfellow2020generative}) to volatility-surface calibration, we use conditional adversarial training for IVS forecasting.  Adversarial training
is a framework within which the Generator and Critic may themselves be convolutional networks.


More specifically, we use a Wasserstein GAN (WGAN; \citealp{arjovsky2017wasserstein}) rather than the original minimax GAN objective.  With an optimal discriminator, the original minimax criterion is linked to Jensen--Shannon divergence, not a general minimization of Kullback--Leibler divergence.  WGAN instead uses a Lipschitz-constrained Critic to target the Wasserstein-1 objective.  This design is intended to provide more informative gradients and improve optimization.


Furthermore, to encourage the generated volatility surfaces to remain consistent with financial theory, we augment the Generator objective with soft penalties for calendar-spread and butterfly-arbitrage violations on the modelling grid. Following \cite{sidogi2022creating}, \cite{vuletic2024volgan}, and \cite{na2023computing}, these penalties are incorporated within the conditional GAN framework (CGAN; \citealp{mirza2014conditional}).


Overall, the integration of conditional information with these economic regularizers is intended to improve predictive accuracy while discouraging irregular surface shapes. The reported diagnostics evaluate discrete violations under the proxy clock defined below; they neither establish nor guarantee global absence of arbitrage at actual contract expiries.

For each forecast origin $t$, the Generator combines the current IVS, its
current-side support mask and fixed grid coordinates, the pair-level LP text
embedding, and a Gaussian noise vector to produce
$\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+h}$, where $h=5$ minutes.

The Critic restricts the current IVS and either the realized future surface or
the Generator forecast to their joint support, concatenates them along the
channel dimension, and returns the scalar score used in the Wasserstein
objective.

The associated change forecast is
\(\widehat{\Delta_h\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_t
=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+h}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t\).
Subtracting the same observed current surface from the prediction and target
leaves their pointwise forecast error unchanged. Thus, the stored
future-surface targets and masked MAE also evaluate the next-window IVS change
defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch3:forecast_information_sets}. The common relative-strike
and business-day-maturity grid describes an option-pricing representation.

\subsection*{Text Embeddings}

Text embeddings transform a variable-length sequence of words or tokens into a fixed-dimensional, dense numerical representation. In contrast to a sparse count vector, the coordinates of a learned embedding are not assigned to individual words, instead, the vector jointly represents linguistic features learned from large text corpora. Contextual language models such as BERT produce token representations that depend on the surrounding words \citep{devlin2019bert}.  A separate pooling or embedding objective is then needed to obtain a fixed-length representation of a sentence or document. For instance, Sentence-BERT learns sentence-level vectors whose semantic proximity can be measured by cosine similarity \citep{reimers2019sentencebert}.


Meanwhile, text representations have been used in financial prediction. For instance, \citet{peng2016leverage} combine financial-news word embeddings with neural networks to predict stock-price movements, while \citet{araci2019finbert} shows that adapting a pretrained language model to financial language improves financial sentiment classification.  More closely related to the volatility setting, \citet{rahimikia2021realised} construct financial word embeddings from business news and use them as inputs to realised-volatility forecasts. This literature indicates that embeddings can preserve information beyond simple word counts, although whether such information improves IVS forecasts is an empirical question examined in this chapter.

The text input used in the main experiments is the lead paragraph (LP) of each Dow Jones news article. The source corpus contains 1,024-dimensional vectors identified in the project records as outputs of OpenAI's \texttt{text-embedding-3-large} model. The model has a native maximum output dimension of 3,072 and permits a shorter 1,024-dimensional representation through its \texttt{dimensions} option \citep{openai2024newembeddingmodels}.
% We also compared the 512- and 1,024-dimensional embeddings in this chapter.


Let $x_a$ denote the LP text of article $a$, and let $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{3L}}(\cdot;d)$ denote the embedding mapping implemented by OpenAI’s \texttt{text-embedding-3-large} model, where \(d\) denotes the output dimension. The article-level representation is written as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:article_lp_embedding}
\widetilde{\mathbf l}_a
=
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{3L}}(x_a;d=1024)
\in\mathbb{R}^{1024}.
\end{equation}


For each eligible forecast origin \(t_p\), we constructed a market pair from
two consecutive \(h\)-minute intervals: the IVS estimated over
\([t_p-h,t_p)\) forms the current input surface, while the IVS estimated over
\([t_p,t_p+h)\) forms the forecast target. Each news article is then assigned,
using its availability timestamp, to the first eligible market pair whose
forecast origin is no earlier than the article's availability and no more than
\(h\) minutes later.


Multiple rows from the source news dataset may be assigned to the same eligible
forecast origin \(t_p\), and hence to the same market pair. Before aggregation,
these news records are deduplicated using their effective article identifiers.
Let \(\mathcal A_p\) be the resulting set of unique articles assigned to pair
\(p\). The model input is the mean article vector followed by \(L_2\) normalisation:



\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:pair_lp_embedding}
\begin{aligned}
\overline{\mathbf l}_p
&=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal A_p|}
\sum_{a\in\mathcal A_p}\widetilde{\mathbf l}_a,\\
\mathbf w_p
&=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle
\frac{\overline{\mathbf l}_p}{\|\overline{\mathbf l}_p\|_2},
& \|\overline{\mathbf l}_p\|_2>0,\\[6pt]
\mathbf 0,
& \|\overline{\mathbf l}_p\|_2=0.
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}


where \(p\) indexes a market--news pair, \(a\) indexes an article, \(\mathcal A_p\) is the set of unique articles assigned to pair \(p\), and \(|\mathcal A_p|\) is the number of articles in that set. The vector \(\widetilde{\mathbf l}_a\in\mathbb{R}^{1024}\) is the article-level LP embedding defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch3:article_lp_embedding}, while \(\overline{\mathbf l}_p\in\mathbb{R}^{1024}\) is the unnormalised arithmetic mean of those embeddings. The operator \(\|\cdot\|_2\) denotes the Euclidean norm, \(\mathbf w_p\in\mathbb{R}^{1024}\) is the resulting \(L_2\)-normalised pair-level representation, and \(\mathbf 0\in\mathbb{R}^{1024}\) is the zero vector assigned when the mean embedding has zero norm.


Deduplication prevents repeated copies of an article from receiving additional weight. Mean aggregation gives every unique article equal weight. The primary purpose of the subsequent \(L_2\) normalisation is to remove variation in the overall magnitude of the aggregated embedding vectors, thereby placing all nonzero pair representations on a common scale. Consequently, the FiLM conditioning network primarily receives differences in embedding direction rather than differences in raw vector magnitude. The resulting $\mathbf w_p\in\mathbb{R}^{1024}$ is passed to the $1024\rightarrow256\rightarrow128$ text adapter in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch3:text_adapter}; the subsequent layer-wise FiLM operations are described with the Generator below.



\subsection*{Non-arbitrage Conditions}

The non-arbitrage conditions require the option prices implied by the IVS to
exhibit economically consistent behaviour across both strikes and maturities.
Let \(C_t^{\mathrm{bl}}(K,T)\) denote the Black--76 (\citet{black76}) European call
value associated with an IVS quote at time \(t\), let
\(\tau=\tau^{\mathrm{act}}(t,T)\) be the ACT/365 time to expiry, let
\(F_t(T)\) be the futures price, and let
\(D_t(T)=e^{-r\tau}\) be the discount factor. For relative strike
\(m=K/F_t(T)\), define the forward- and discount-normalised call value as

\begin{equation}
c(m,\tau)
=
\frac{C_t^{\mathrm{bl}}\!\left(mF_t(T),T\right)}
     {D_t(T)F_t(T)}.
\label{eq:ch3:normalised_call_definition}
\end{equation}

Within this normalised Black representation, the relevant shape restrictions
can be written in a common coordinate system:

\begin{align}
\frac{\partial c(m,\tau)}{\partial m}
&\leq 0,
&& \text{(strike monotonicity)},
\label{eq:ch3:monotonicity}\\
\frac{\partial^2 c(m,\tau)}{\partial m^2}
&\geq 0,
&& \text{(strike convexity)},
\label{eq:ch3:convexity}\\
\frac{\partial c(m,\tau)}{\partial \tau}
&\geq 0,
&& \text{(calendar monotonicity)}.
\label{eq:ch3:calendar}
\end{align}

The first condition rules out negative vertical-spread values, the second
rules out negative butterfly-spread values and implies a non-negative
risk-neutral density, and the third rules out calendar-spread violations in
the normalised representation \citep{breedenlitzenberger1978,gatheral2014arbitragefree}.
Moreover, because the normalised Black call value is increasing in total
implied variance at fixed \(m\), the calendar condition has the equivalent
representation

\begin{equation}
w(m,\tau)
=
\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}^2(m,\tau)\tau,
\qquad
\frac{\partial w(m,\tau)}{\partial \tau}\geq 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{\partial c(m,\tau)}{\partial \tau}\geq 0.
\label{eq:ch3:totalvariance}
\end{equation}

Noticeably, the observed Treasury futures options are American-style contracts. The
conditions above are therefore operational shape restrictions on the
European Black--76-equivalent IVS representation used by the model, rather
than sufficient no-arbitrage conditions imposed directly on the observed
American option prices.


The generated IV
\(\widehat{\sigma}_{b,j,i}=[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{b,t+h}]_{j,i}\)
is mapped to a synthetic normalised Black call value using this proxy:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\sigma}^{\epsilon}_{b,j,i}
&=\max\!\left(\widehat{\sigma}_{b,j,i},10^{-4}\right),\\
\widehat d_{1,b,j,i}
&=
\frac{-\log m_i
      +\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\widehat{\sigma}^{\epsilon}_{b,j,i}\right)^2\widetilde{\tau}_j}
     {\widehat{\sigma}^{\epsilon}_{b,j,i}\sqrt{\widetilde{\tau}_j}},\\
\widehat d_{2,b,j,i}
&=\widehat d_{1,b,j,i}
  -\widehat{\sigma}^{\epsilon}_{b,j,i}\sqrt{\widetilde{\tau}_j},\\
\widehat c_{b,j,i}
&=\Phi\!\left(\widehat d_{1,b,j,i}\right)
  -m_i\Phi\!\left(\widehat d_{2,b,j,i}\right),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ch3:normalised_black_call}
\end{equation}

where \(\Phi(\cdot)\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. The numerical floors on implied volatility and time to maturity match the executable specification and prevent division by zero. 


In particular, \(\widehat c_{b,j,i}\) does not reprice a specific contract at
its actual expiry. It is a synthetic normalized Black call value constructed
from the fixed relative-strike and maturity grids. The implemented calendar
penalty acts on adjacent-maturity differences in these synthetic call values:

\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathcal L}_{\mathrm{cal}}
=
\frac{1}{B}
\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\frac{1}{(N_T-1)N_m}
\sum_{j=1}^{N_T-1}
\sum_{i=1}^{N_m}
\left[
\widehat c_{b,j,i}
-
\widehat c_{b,j+1,i}
\right]_{+}.
\end{equation}

Thus, the penalty discourages decreases in synthetic normalized call value across adjacent maturity-grid points. It is a soft grid-shape restriction and should not be interpreted as a sufficient test of calendar arbitrage in the underlying traded contracts.

The implemented calendar penalty is evaluated over all adjacent maturity points of the synthetic grid. It does not apply the joint-support mask internally. Specifically,

\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{cal}}
=
\frac{1}{B}
\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\frac{1}{(N_T-1)N_m}
\sum_{j=1}^{N_T-1}
\sum_{i=1}^{N_m}
\left[
\widehat c_{b,j,i}
-
\widehat c_{b,j+1,i}
\right]_{+},
\label{eq:ch3:calendar_loss}
\end{equation}

where \([x]_{+}=\max(x,0)\). Hence, only decreases in the synthetic normalized call value across adjacent maturity-grid points contribute to the penalty. Because the normalized Black call value is monotone in total variance at a fixed relative strike, this criterion identifies the same direction of calendar violation as a proxy-total-variance comparison. However, its penalty magnitude is measured in normalized-call-value units rather than total-variance units.

The butterfly penalty is

\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{bfly}}
=
\frac{1}{B}
\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\frac{
\sum_{j=1}^{N_T}
\sum_{i=2}^{N_m-1}
H_{b,j,i}
\left[
-\left(
\widehat c_{b,j,i+1}
-2\widehat c_{b,j,i}
+\widehat c_{b,j,i-1}
\right)
\right]_{+}}
{\max\!\left(1,\sum_{j=1}^{N_T}\sum_{i=2}^{N_m-1}H_{b,j,i}\right)}.
\label{eq:ch3:butterfly_loss}
\end{equation}


This term penalises negative second differences and is therefore zero when the sampled call-price slice is convex at every interior moneyness point. Both terms are soft, finite-grid regularizers, they reduce the mean magnitude of sampled calendar and butterfly violations. Neither penalty is multiplied by the support mask; both are evaluated over the full generated grid. Moreover, because maturity slices may reference different underlying Treasury futures contracts, \(\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{cal}}\) is interpreted as a calendar-shape regularizer for the normalised IVS rather than as a sufficient test of a directly tradable calendar spread. 







\subsection*{Wasserstein Generative Adversarial Network (WGAN) with Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) Layers}

To predict the Implied Volatility Surface (IVS), we applied a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN, \citealp{goodfellow2020generative}) with the Wasserstein-1 distance (WGAN, \citealp{arjovsky2017wasserstein}) in the loss function during training.

An Implied Volatility Surface can be represented naturally as a two-dimensional
grid indexed by relative strike and time to maturity. Throughout this chapter,
\(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_T\times N_m}\) denotes the IVS at
time \(t\), with rows indexed by business-day maturities
\(q_1,\ldots,q_{N_T}\) and columns indexed by relative-strike levels
\(m_1,\ldots,m_{N_m}\). Its \((j,i)\)th element is
\([\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t]_{j,i}=\sigma_t(m_i,q_j)\).


% WGAN
Meanwhile, the GAN model takes the current distribution and random noise as input, the target distribution as output. The model consists of two parts, a Generator designed to predict target surface, a Critic worked as a judge qualifying the output of the generator.\footnote{Following the terminology of Wasserstein GANs, we use the term ``critic'' rather than ``discriminator'', because the network produces a real-valued score instead of a probability of an observation being real.} \cite{arjovsky2017wasserstein} modified the model by training a real-valued critic to approximate the Wasserstein-1 distance, which is called the Wasserstein GAN (WGAN). The distance supplies an informative ordering of generated samples without interpreting the critic output as a probability and is therefore better suited to continuous, high-dimensional IVSs.

In this research, the generator is designed to produce a predicted IVS conditional on the available market information, whereas the critic evaluates the consistency of the generated surface with the empirical distribution of observed IVSs. Through the adversarial training process, the generator learns to produce volatility surfaces that are difficult for the critic to distinguish from their realized counterparts.


Moreover, the flexible construction of the generator and discriminator allows our taking advantage of the Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in local feature extraction.


The CNNs development from the neocognitron \citep{fukushima1980neocognitron} to trainable convolutional architectures \citep{lecun1989backpropagation,lecun1998gradient} established the usefulness of local receptive fields and shared kernels for structured grids. These properties are directly relevant to an IVS: variation along moneyness describes the smile, skew, and curvature, whereas variation along maturity describes the term structure. Empirical IVS forecasting studies similarly show that convolutional models can preserve and exploit these local spatial dependencies \citep{medvedev2022multistep,shao2025prediction}. We therefore use a U-Net-style encoder--decoder network with skip connections \citep{ronneberger2015unet} so that increasingly abstract representations can be learned without discarding fine-scale surface information.


% FiLM
Furthermore, to allow text available at the forecast origin to condition the subsequent IVS prediction, we incorporate Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM, \citep{perez2018film}) layers into the Generator. FiLM was originally introduced as a general-purpose conditioning mechanism for visual reasoning, in which representations extracted from a natural-language question generate feature-specific scaling and shifting parameters that modulate intermediate visual features. Unlike simple input concatenation, FiLM allows the conditioning information to affect neural-network representations at multiple layers through feature-wise affine transformations. Also, layer-wise modulation allows the same textual signal to alter representations at several spatial scales.



Subsequent studies have demonstrated that FiLM provides a flexible and computationally efficient mechanism for integrating auxiliary information across a broad range of applications. For instance, in multimodal learning, feature-wise conditioning has been used to incorporate linguistic information into image colorization \citep{manjunatha2018learning} and to perform conditional style transfer \citep{dumoulin2016learned}. Related conditioning mechanisms have also been applied to medical image segmentation, where patient- and image-level metadata are used to modulate intermediate feature maps \citep{lemay2021benefits}, and to text-conditioned generative models, such as latent diffusion models for audio generation \citep{liu2023audioldm}.


Recent studies have further applied FiLM-based conditioning to financial generative modelling. For example, \citet{kubiak2025macrovae} use FiLM within convolutional residual blocks to generate asset-return sequences conditional on macroeconomic scenarios, while \citet{wang2025diffvolume} employ FiLM-type conditioning in a diffusion model for limit-order-book volume generation. These applications provide additional motivation for using FiLM to incorporate text-based conditioning information into the generation of financial surfaces.



Overall, the flexibility of FiLM is particularly relevant to our setting because financial text embeddings and IVS representations constitute two heterogeneous sources of information. The text embedding summarizes contextual information concerning market expectations, uncertainty, sentiment, and firm-specific events, whereas the hidden representations of the generator encode the level, slope, curvature, and local dynamics of the volatility surface. By using the text embedding to generate layer-specific scaling and shifting parameters, FiLM allows textual information to selectively amplify or suppress different latent IVS features rather than imposing a homogeneous effect on the entire surface.




Given the flexibility of FiLM in multimodal tasks, particularly in text-conditioned generation, and drawing on the framework proposed by \citet{dumoulin2018feature}, we employ a convolutional generator augmented with FiLM layers. The complete network architecture is described below within the generator-critic framework.



%GAN
\subsubsection*{FiLM-CNN Generator}
Generative adversarial networks (GANs), introduced by \citet{goodfellow2014generative}, consist of two parameterized neural networks: a generator, denoted by $G(\cdot;\theta_g)$, and a discriminator, denoted by $D(\cdot;\theta_d)$, where $\theta_g$ and $\theta_d$ represent their respective sets of trainable parameters. The two networks are trained within an adversarial minimax framework. Specifically, the generator induces a model distribution that is intended to approximate the empirical data distribution, whereas the discriminator is trained to distinguish samples produced by the generator from observations drawn from the empirical distribution. Through iterative adversarial training, the generator progressively learns to produce outputs that reproduce the salient distributional characteristics of the observed data.


We first specify the generator component. In the executable specification,
\(N_T=N_m=16\), and the forecasting target is
\(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t+h}\) with \(h=5\) minutes. Let
\(\mathbf{w}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{1024}\) be the corresponding LP text
representation and let
\(\mathbf{z}_t\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{32})\) be a 32-dimensional
latent innovation. The proposed model produces


\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:generator}
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+h}
=G_{\theta}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t,
\mathbf{M}_t,
\mathbf{C}_{m},
\mathbf{C}_{\log \tau},
\mathbf{w}_t,
\mathbf{z}_t
\right),
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{M}_t$ is a binary support-indicator matrix derived solely from the raw option observations available at time \(t\). An entry of 1 indicates that the corresponding grid cell is supported by the current raw surface, whereas an entry of 0 indicates that it lacks current-side raw-data support. The masked matrix is important in the loss, critic, and evaluation to prevent unsupported or interpolation-dependent regions from distorting the learning objective and performance metrics.

\(\mathbf{C}_{m}\) and \(\mathbf{C}_{\log \tau}\) are fixed coordinate maps for
relative strike and time to maturity. Both coordinates are normalised to
\([-1,1]\) using min--max normalisation:


\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:surface_coordinates}
\begin{aligned}
\left[\mathbf{C}_{m}\right]_{j,i}
&=2\frac{m_i-m_{\min}}{m_{\max}-m_{\min}}-1,\\
[\mathbf{C}_{\log \tau}]_{j,i}
&=2\frac{\log q_j-\log q_{\min}}
{\log q_{\max}-\log q_{\min}}-1.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\paragraph*{CNN construction.}
The Generator ($G(\cdot)$) employs a fully convolutional U-Net architecture to preserve the two-dimensional structure of the implied volatility surface. Its convolutional backbone contains seven trainable convolutional layers ($\ell=7$): three encoder convolutions ($E(\cdot)$), one bottleneck convolution ($B(\cdot)$), two decoder convolutions ($D(\cdot)$), and one pointwise residual-output convolution ($R(\cdot)$). The encoder uses two strided convolutions to transform the four-channel $16\times16$ input into increasingly abstract representations of sizes $32\times16\times16$,
$64\times8\times8$, and $128\times4\times4$. At the bottleneck, a spatially
broadcast Gaussian latent vector is incorporated into the deepest surface
representation. The decoder then restores the original $16\times16$ resolution
using bilinear upsampling and two skip connections from the encoder, thereby
combining global surface information with fine-scale moneyness--maturity
features. Finally, a $1\times1$ convolution produces a residual field relative
to the current IVS.




\paragraph*{CNN Encoder.}

At encoder stage $E_{\ell}$, a two-dimensional convolution is applied over the
moneyness--maturity grid. Each encoder stage comprises three sequential
operations: convolutional feature extraction, FiLM conditioning using the
encoded text representation, and LeakyReLU nonlinear activation.

Accordingly, the input feature map is first convolved, the resulting channel-wise features are then scaled and shifted by FiLM, and the modulated features are finally passed through the LeakyReLU activation function.

The pre-FiLM convolutional feature map ($\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}_{E,t}$) is given by

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:encoder_convolution}
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}_{E,t}
=
\mathbf{K}^{(\ell)}_{E}
\ast
\mathbf{E}^{(\ell-1)}_t
+
\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}_{E},
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}_{E,t}$ denotes the intermediate feature map produced by the convolution at encoder layer $E_{\ell}$ before FiLM modulation and nonlinear activation. $\mathbf{E}^{(\ell-1)}_t$ denotes the activated output feature map from the preceding encoder layer, while $\mathbf{K}^{(\ell)}_{E}$ and $\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}_{E}$ denote the trainable convolutional kernel and bias vector of encoder layer $E_{\ell}$, respectively. The operator $\ast$ denotes two-dimensional convolution over the moneyness--maturity grid. For the first encoder layer, $\mathbf{E}^{(0)}_t=\mathbf{X}^{(0)}_t$ denotes the four-channel Generator input.


More explicitly, the pre-FiLM convolutional output at channel $c$ and grid
cell $(j,i)$ is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:encoder_convolution_element}
H^{(\ell)}_{E,t,c,j,i}
=
b^{(\ell)}_{E,c}
+
\sum_{c'=1}^{C_{\ell-1}}
\sum_{u=-1}^{1}
\sum_{v=-1}^{1}
K^{(\ell)}_{E,c,c',u,v}
\,
\overline{E}^{(\ell-1)}_
{t,c',(j-1)s_\ell+u+1,(i-1)s_\ell+v+1},
\end{equation}

where $C_{\ell-1}$ denotes the number of input channels, $s_\ell$ denotes the stride, and $\overline{\mathbf{E}}^{(\ell-1)}_t$ denotes the zero-padded input feature map. The quantities $\mathbf{K}^{(\ell)}_E$ and $\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}_E$ are the trainable convolutional kernel and bias of encoder layer $E_\ell$, respectively. All encoder convolutions use $3\times3$ kernels with unit padding. Consequently, a stride of one preserves the spatial resolution, whereas a stride of two halves both spatial dimensions for the even-sized grids used in this architecture.


The increasing channel width allows the network to learn progressively richer surface representations, while the decreasing spatial resolution enlarges the effective receptive field across the moneyness--maturity grid.

\paragraph*{CNN Bottleneck.}
At the deepest encoder resolution, the feature map $\mathbf{E}^{(3)}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times128\times4\times4}$ contains the most compressed representation of the current IVS. To introduce stochastic variation into the forecast, the latent vector $\mathbf{z}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{32}$ is spatially broadcast over the $4\times4$ bottleneck grid:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:bottleneck_noise_broadcast}
\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_t
=
\operatorname{Broadcast}_{4\times4}(\mathbf{z}_t)
\in
\mathbb{R}^{B\times32\times4\times4}.
\end{equation}

The broadcast latent representation is concatenated with the deepest encoder feature map along the channel dimension:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:bottleneck_concatenation}
\mathbf{U}_{B,t}
=
\operatorname{Concat}_{\mathrm{ch}}
\left[
\mathbf{E}^{(3)}_t;
\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_t
\right]
\in
\mathbb{R}^{B\times160\times4\times4}.
\end{equation}

A $3\times3$ convolution with stride one and unit padding then maps the
concatenated 160-channel representation back to 128 channels:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:bottleneck_convolution}
\mathbf{H}_{B,t}
=
\mathbf{K}_{B}
\ast
\mathbf{U}_{B,t}
+
\mathbf{b}_{B},
\qquad
\mathbf{H}_{B,t}
\in
\mathbb{R}^{B\times128\times4\times4},
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{K}_{B}$ and $\mathbf{b}_{B}$ denote the trainable convolutional
kernel and bias of the bottleneck, respectively. The resulting feature map
$\mathbf{H}_{B,t}$ is subsequently conditioned by the encoded text
representation and passed through LeakyReLU using the FiLM operation defined
below, producing the final bottleneck representation $\mathbf{B}_t$.


\paragraph*{CNN Decoder.}
The decoder progressively restores the spatial resolution of the bottleneck
representation. At decoder stage $D_{\ell}$, the deeper representation is
first bilinearly upsampled to the resolution of the corresponding encoder
feature map. The upsampled representation is then concatenated with the
encoder skip feature and processed by a $3\times3$ convolution.

At decoder stage $D_1$, the bottleneck representation
$\mathbf{B}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times128\times4\times4}$ is bilinearly
upsampled to an $8\times8$ grid and concatenated with the second encoder
feature map
$\mathbf{E}^{(2)}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times64\times8\times8}$:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:decoder1_convolution}
\mathbf{D}^{(1)}_{t,\mathrm{conv}}
=
\mathbf{K}^{(1)}_D
\ast
\operatorname{Concat}_{\mathrm{ch}}
\left[
\operatorname{Up}_{8\times8}(\mathbf{B}_t);
\mathbf{E}^{(2)}_t
\right]
+
\mathbf{b}^{(1)}_D,
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{D}^{(1)}_{t,\mathrm{conv}}
\in
\mathbb{R}^{B\times64\times8\times8}.
\end{equation}

The concatenated input contains $128+64=192$ channels, and the convolution
maps these 192 channels into 64 output channels.


At decoder stage $D_2$, the output of $D_1$ is bilinearly upsampled to the
original $16\times16$ resolution and concatenated with the first encoder
feature map
$\mathbf{E}^{(1)}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times32\times16\times16}$:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:decoder2_convolution}
\mathbf{D}^{(2)}_{t,\mathrm{conv}}
=
\mathbf{K}^{(2)}_D
\ast
\operatorname{Concat}_{\mathrm{ch}}
\left[
\operatorname{Up}_{16\times16}
\left(
\mathbf{D}^{(1)}_t
\right);
\mathbf{E}^{(1)}_t
\right]
+
\mathbf{b}^{(2)}_D,
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{D}^{(2)}_{t,\mathrm{conv}}
\in
\mathbb{R}^{B\times32\times16\times16}.
\end{equation}

The concatenated input contains $64+32=96$ channels, and the convolution maps
these 96 channels into 32 output channels. The resulting feature map is
subsequently conditioned through FiLM and passed through LeakyReLU before
being supplied to the residual-output head.

\paragraph*{FiLM.}
Textual information enters the Generator $G(\cdot)$ exclusively through
Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM), denoted by
$\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}(\cdot)$ at stage $\ell$. Specifically, the aggregated
LP representation $\mathbf{w}_t$ is first transformed into a
128-dimensional text vector $\mathbf{e}_t$. This encoded representation
determines a separate set of channel-wise FiLM parameters for each of the
three encoder stages, the bottleneck, and the two decoder stages. Text can
therefore condition the convolutional representations at multiple spatial
resolutions without being directly concatenated with the surface feature
maps.

The two-layer text adapter is defined as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:text_adapter}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{h}_t
&=
\phi\left[
\operatorname{LayerNorm}
\left(
\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{w}_t+\mathbf{b}_1
\right)
\right],\\
\mathbf{e}_t
&=
\phi\left[
\mathbf{W}_2
\operatorname{Dropout}_{0.1}
\left(
\mathbf{h}_t
\right)
+
\mathbf{b}_2
\right],
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{w}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{1024}$ denotes the aggregated LP text
embedding and $\phi$ denotes the LeakyReLU activation function with a negative
slope of $0.2$. The matrices
$\mathbf{W}_1\in\mathbb{R}^{256\times1024}$ and
$\mathbf{W}_2\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times256}$, together with their corresponding
bias vectors $\mathbf{b}_1\in\mathbb{R}^{256}$ and
$\mathbf{b}_2\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$, are the trainable parameters of the two
linear transformations. The first transformation maps the original LP
embedding into a 256-dimensional hidden representation, while the second
produces the conditioning vector
$\mathbf{e}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$ used by all FiLM stages. Layer
normalization has its own trainable affine scale and bias parameters, while
dropout is active only during training.

Let

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:film_stage_set}
\mathcal{S}
=
\left\{
E_1,E_2,E_3,B,D_1,D_2
\right\}
\end{equation}

denote the six FiLM-conditioned stages. Their respective output-channel
dimensions are

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:film_stage_channels}
\left(
C_{E_1},
C_{E_2},
C_{E_3},
C_B,
C_{D_1},
C_{D_2}
\right)
=
\left(
32,64,128,128,64,32
\right).
\end{equation}

For a generic stage $\ell\in\mathcal{S}$ with $C_\ell$ output channels, a
stage-specific linear projection maps $\mathbf{e}_t$ into a
$2C_\ell$-dimensional parameter vector:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:film_parameters}
\mathbf{q}^{(\ell)}_t
=
\mathcal{P}^{(\ell)}
\left(
\mathbf{e}_t
\right)
=
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{FiLM}}^{(\ell)}
\mathbf{e}_t
+
\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{FiLM}}^{(\ell)}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(\ell)}_t\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(\ell)}_t
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Here, $\mathcal{P}^{(\ell)}(\cdot)$ denotes the parameter projection associated
with the FiLM operation at stage $\ell$, while
$\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{FiLM}}^{(\ell)}
\in\mathbb{R}^{2C_\ell\times128}$ and
$\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{FiLM}}^{(\ell)}
\in\mathbb{R}^{2C_\ell}$ denote its trainable weight matrix and bias vector,
respectively. The projection output
$\mathbf{q}^{(\ell)}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{2C_\ell}$ is divided into two equal
parts:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:film_parameter_split}
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(\ell)}_t
=
\left[
\mathbf{q}^{(\ell)}_t
\right]_{1:C_\ell},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(\ell)}_t
=
\left[
\mathbf{q}^{(\ell)}_t
\right]_{C_\ell+1:2C_\ell}.
\end{equation}

The vectors
$\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(\ell)}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{C_\ell}$ and
$\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(\ell)}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{C_\ell}$ represent the
text-conditioned channel-wise scale adjustment and additive shift,
respectively.

Let $\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}_t$ denote the convolutional feature map at stage
$\ell$ before text conditioning. The complete FiLM transformation is defined
as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:film_transformation}
\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}^{(\ell)}_t
=
\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}
\left(
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}_t;
\mathbf{e}_t
\right)
=
\left(
\mathbf{1}
+
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(\ell)}_t
\right)
\odot
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}_t
+
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(\ell)}_t.
\end{equation}

The semicolon in $\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}_t;\mathbf{e}_t)$
distinguishes the convolutional feature map being modulated from the encoded
text vector that provides the conditioning information. The vectors
$\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(\ell)}_t$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(\ell)}_t$ are broadcast across the moneyness and maturity dimensions. Equivalently, for channel $c$ and grid cell $(j,i)$,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:film_transformation_element}
\widetilde{H}^{(\ell)}_{t,c,j,i}
=
\left(
1+\gamma^{(\ell)}_{t,c}
\right)
H^{(\ell)}_{t,c,j,i}
+
\beta^{(\ell)}_{t,c}.
\end{equation}

The FiLM-modulated feature map is then passed through the LeakyReLU activation
function, denoted by $\phi(\cdot)$:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:film_activation}
\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}_t
=
\phi\left[
\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}
\left(
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}_t;
\mathbf{e}_t
\right)
\right]
=
\phi\left(
\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}^{(\ell)}_t
\right).
\end{equation}

Depending on the position of stage $\ell$ within the Generator,
$\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}_t$ is denoted by
$\mathbf{E}^{(r)}_t$ for encoder stage $E_r$,
$\mathbf{B}_t$ for the bottleneck, or
$\mathbf{D}^{(r)}_t$ for decoder stage $D_r$. In particular,
$\mathbf{E}^{(1)}_t$ and $\mathbf{E}^{(2)}_t$ are retained as U-Net skip
features, while $\mathbf{D}^{(2)}_t$ is passed to the residual-output head.

All FiLM projection weights and biases are initialized to zero:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:film_identity_initialization}
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{FiLM}}^{(\ell)}
=
\mathbf{0},
\qquad
\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{FiLM}}^{(\ell)}
=
\mathbf{0}.
\end{equation}

Consequently,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:film_initial_parameters}
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(\ell)}_t
=
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(\ell)}_t
=
\mathbf{0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}
\left(
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}_t;
\mathbf{e}_t
\right)
=
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}_t.
\end{equation}

Each FiLM transformation therefore acts as an identity mapping at
initialization. Together with the zero-initialized residual-output head
described below, this produces a staged gradient start-up. At the exact
initial state, the zero residual-head weights prevent gradients from reaching
the convolutional backbone, the FiLM projections, and the text adapter. The
first Generator update therefore changes only the residual-head parameters.
Once the residual-head weights become non-zero, effective gradients can reach
the convolutional backbone and the FiLM projections. Gradients reach the
upstream text adapter only after the FiLM projection weights have themselves
moved away from zero.



%%%%%
\paragraph*{LeakyReLU.}
Each convolutional feature map is passed through the Leaky Rectified Linear
Unit (LeakyReLU) after FiLM conditioning. The activation function is applied
element-wise and is defined as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:leaky_relu}
\phi(x)
=
\begin{cases}
x, & x\geq 0,\\
\alpha x, & x<0,
\end{cases}
\qquad
\alpha=0.2,
\end{equation}

where $\alpha$ denotes the negative slope. Unlike the standard ReLU, which
sets all negative inputs to zero, LeakyReLU retains a small negative response.
Its derivative is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:leaky_relu_derivative}
\frac{\partial\phi(x)}{\partial x}
=
\begin{cases}
1, & x>0,\\
\alpha, & x<0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

The non-zero gradient in the negative region reduces the risk of permanently
inactive features and allows both positive and negative deviations in the
convolutional representations to remain informative during training. This is
particularly relevant after FiLM because the additive shift
$\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(\ell)}_t$ may move individual feature channels into
either the positive or negative region.

For a generic FiLM-conditioned stage $\ell$, the activated output is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:film_leaky_relu_output}
\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}_t
=
\phi\left[
\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}
\left(
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}_t;
\mathbf{e}_t
\right)
\right]
=
\phi\left[
\left(
\mathbf{1}
+
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(\ell)}_t
\right)
\odot
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}_t
+
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(\ell)}_t
\right].
\end{equation}

Depending on the location of stage $\ell$ within the Generator,
$\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}_t$ corresponds to an encoder output
$\mathbf{E}^{(r)}_t$, the bottleneck output $\mathbf{B}_t$, or a decoder
output $\mathbf{D}^{(r)}_t$. LeakyReLU is applied after all six FiLM
operations: the three encoder stages, the bottleneck, and the two decoder
stages. It is also used in both layers of the text adapter. It is not applied
to the final $1\times1$ residual-output convolution, whose output is instead
processed by the identity-anchored softplus transformation.



%%%%%%

\paragraph*{Residual-output Head.}
The output of the second decoder stage,
$\mathbf{D}^{(2)}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times32\times16\times16}$, is mapped
to a single-channel residual volatility field using a pointwise
$1\times1$ convolution:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:residual_head}
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_t
=
\mathbf{K}_{R}
\ast
\mathbf{D}^{(2)}_t
+
\mathbf{b}_{R},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_t
\in
\mathbb{R}^{B\times1\times16\times16},
\end{equation}

where
$\mathbf{K}_{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times32\times1\times1}$ and
$\mathbf{b}_{R}\in\mathbb{R}$ denote the trainable kernel and bias of the
residual-output head, respectively. Because the convolution has a
$1\times1$ kernel, it combines the 32 decoder channels independently at each
moneyness--maturity grid cell without mixing information between neighbouring
cells. No FiLM or LeakyReLU operation is applied after this convolution.

The resulting field $\boldsymbol{\Delta}_t$ is not interpreted as the future
IVS directly. Instead, it represents a displacement from the current
unmasked surface $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t$. To preserve positivity while making
the initial forecast exactly equal to the persistence forecast, the model uses
an identity-anchored softplus transformation. Define the unconstrained anchor
coordinate as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:residual_anchor}
\mathbf{A}_t
=
\operatorname{softplus}^{-1}
\left[
\max
\left\{
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t-\varepsilon,
\xi\mathbf{1}
\right\}
\right],
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:softplus_definition}
\operatorname{softplus}(x)
=
\log\left(1+\exp(x)\right),
\end{equation}

$\varepsilon=10^{-4}$ is the positive-volatility offset, and $\xi>0$ is a
small numerical floor that ensures that the inverse softplus remains
well-defined. The predicted future IVS is then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:identity_residual}
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+h}
=
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t
+
\operatorname{softplus}
\left(
\mathbf{A}_t+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_t
\right)
-
\operatorname{softplus}
\left(
\mathbf{A}_t
\right).
\end{equation}

Both the kernel $\mathbf{K}_{R}$ and the bias $\mathbf{b}_{R}$ are initialized
to zero. Consequently,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:residual_identity_initialization}
\mathbf{K}_{R}
=
\mathbf{0},
\qquad
\mathbf{b}_{R}
=
\mathbf{0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_t
=
\mathbf{0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+h}
=
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t.
\end{equation}

The Generator therefore begins from the persistence forecast rather than from
an arbitrary randomly generated surface. For valid volatility inputs satisfying
$\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t>\varepsilon$ element-wise, the transformation also
ensures that the generated surface remains above the positive offset
$\varepsilon$, even when the learned residual is negative.

\subsubsection*{FiLM-CNN Critic}

The Critic evaluates a transition from the current IVS
$\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t$ to a candidate future surface $\mathbf{S}$, where

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{S}
\in
\left\{
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t+h},
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+h}
\right\}.
\end{equation}

The realized and generated transitions are evaluated only over cells supported
by both the current and future raw surfaces. The joint-support mask is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:critic_joint_support_mask}
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{t,t+h}
=
\mathbf{M}_t
\odot
\mathbf{M}_{t+h}.
\end{equation}

The Critic input is formed by masking the current and candidate surfaces
separately and concatenating them along the channel dimension:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:nolp_critic_input}
\mathbf{X}^{C}_t(\mathbf{S})
=
\operatorname{Concat}_{\mathrm{ch}}
\left[
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{t,t+h}
\odot
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t;
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{t,t+h}
\odot
\mathbf{S}
\right]
\in
\mathbb{R}^{B\times2\times16\times16}.
\end{equation}

The joint-support mask is used for deterministic zero-masking before the
Critic is evaluated. It is not supplied as an additional Critic channel.
Similarly, the moneyness and log-maturity coordinate maps used by the
Generator are not supplied to the Critic. Thus, the executable Critic score
can be represented as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:nolp_critic}
C_{\psi}
\left(
\mathbf{S},
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t;
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{t,t+h}
\right)
=
c_{\psi}
\left(
\mathbf{X}^{C}_t(\mathbf{S})
\right)
\in\mathbb{R},
\end{equation}

where the semicolon indicates that the joint-support mask determines the
zero-masking operation but is not an independently learned input channel.


\paragraph*{CNN surface encoder.}
The two-channel Critic input is processed by three $3\times3$ convolutions
with stride two and unit padding. The resulting sequence of feature-map
dimensions is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:critic_cnn_dimensions}
2\times16\times16
\longrightarrow
32\times8\times8
\longrightarrow
64\times4\times4
\longrightarrow
128\times2\times2.
\end{equation}

The first convolution maps two input channels to 32 channels and is followed
by LeakyReLU. The second convolution maps 32 channels to 64 channels, while
the third maps 64 channels to 128 channels. The latter two convolutional
stages are followed by affine instance normalization and LeakyReLU with a
negative slope of $0.2$. No residual blocks are used in the Critic. Flattening
the final $128\times2\times2$ representation produces the 512-dimensional
surface feature vector

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:critic_surface_features}
\mathbf{f}^{C}_t(\mathbf{S})
=
f^{C}_{\mathrm{CNN}}
\left(
\mathbf{X}^{C}_t(\mathbf{S})
\right)
\in
\mathbb{R}^{512}.
\end{equation}


The encoded text feature is replaced by an exact zero vector\footnote{The executable implementation retains a zero-valued text branch solely to
preserve the parameter dimensions of the LP-enabled Critic; algebraically,
the resulting score is equivalent to a surface-only Critic.} in the forward pass:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:nolp_zero_text}
\overline{\mathbf{e}}^{C}_t
=
\mathbf{0}
+
0\cdot
f^{C}_{\mathrm{text}}
\left(
\mathbf{w}_t
\right)
=
\mathbf{0}
\in
\mathbb{R}^{128}.
\end{equation}

The 512-dimensional surface representation is concatenated with this
128-dimensional zero vector:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:critic_feature_concatenation}
\mathbf{a}^{C}_t(\mathbf{S})
=
\operatorname{Concat}
\left[
\mathbf{f}^{C}_t(\mathbf{S});
\overline{\mathbf{e}}^{C}_t
\right]
\in
\mathbb{R}^{640}.
\end{equation}

The scalar scoring head is then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:critic_scoring_head}
C_{\psi}
\left(
\mathbf{S},
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t;
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{t,t+h}
\right)
=
\mathbf{W}^{C}_2
\phi
\left(
\mathbf{W}^{C}_1
\mathbf{a}^{C}_t(\mathbf{S})
+
\mathbf{b}^{C}_1
\right)
+
\mathbf{b}^{C}_2,
\end{equation}

where the first linear layer maps $640\rightarrow786$, LeakyReLU is applied
to the resulting hidden representation, and the second linear layer maps
$786\rightarrow1$. No sigmoid activation is applied to the final score.






\subsubsection*{Wasserstein Loss}

In the original GAN framework, the discriminator, denoted by
$D_{\theta_d}(\cdot)$, produces an output between zero and one. This output
can be interpreted as the estimated probability that an observation originates
from the empirical data distribution rather than from the Generator. The
discriminator does not directly minimize the pointwise difference between a
generated observation and its realized counterpart. Instead, it learns to
distinguish the distributional characteristics of empirical and generated
observations.

In a conventional conditional GAN, both the Generator and the discriminator
receive conditioning information $\mathbf{c}_t$. The corresponding minimax
objective is

\begin{align}
\min_{\theta_g}\max_{\theta_d}\,
\mathcal{V}
\left(
D_{\theta_d},
G_{\theta_g}
\right)
={}&
\mathbb{E}_{
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t+h},\mathbf{c}_t)
\sim P_{\mathrm{data}}
}
\left[
\log
D_{\theta_d}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t+h},
\mathbf{c}_t
\right)
\right]
\nonumber\\
&+
\mathbb{E}_{
\mathbf{c}_t\sim P_{\mathbf{c}},
\,\mathbf{z}_t\sim P_z
}
\left[
\log
\left(
1-
D_{\theta_d}
\left(
G_{\theta_g}
\left(
\mathbf{c}_t,\mathbf{z}_t
\right),
\mathbf{c}_t
\right)
\right)
\right],
\label{eq:ch3:cgan_objective}
\end{align}

where $P_{\mathrm{data}}$ denotes the empirical joint distribution,
$P_{\mathbf{c}}$ denotes the empirical marginal distribution of the
conditioning information, and $P_z$ denotes the predefined distribution of
the latent vector $\mathbf{z}_t$.

The classification-based objective in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch3:cgan_objective} may produce unstable or weak gradients when
the empirical and generated distributions have limited overlap. To address
this issue, \citet{arjovsky2017wasserstein} proposed the Wasserstein GAN
(WGAN), which replaces binary classification with the Wasserstein-1 distance.
Following WGAN terminology, the discriminator is therefore replaced by a
real-valued \textit{critic}, denoted by $C_{\psi}(\cdot)$. The Critic output
is neither a probability nor a calibrated measure of forecast accuracy.
Instead, a larger score indicates that the Critic regards a transition as
being more consistent with the empirical transition distribution.

In the implemented model, the generated future IVS is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:wgan_generated_surface}
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+h}
=
G_{\theta}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t,
\mathbf{M}_t,
\mathbf{C}_m,
\mathbf{C}_{\log \tau},
\mathbf{w}_t,
\mathbf{z}_t
\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{z}_t\sim P_z,
\end{equation}

The Critic evaluates either the realized or generated future surface jointly with the current IVS:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{S}_{t+h}
\in
\left\{
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t+h},
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+h}
\right\}.
\end{equation}

Both the current and candidate future surfaces are zero-masked using the
joint-support mask

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{t,t+h}
=
\mathbf{M}_t
\odot
\mathbf{M}_{t+h}
\end{equation}

before being supplied to the Critic. The resulting score is denoted by

\begin{equation}
C_{\psi}
\left(
\mathbf{S}_{t+h},
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t;
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{t,t+h}
\right)
\in
\mathbb{R}.
\end{equation}

Under the Kantorovich--Rubinstein dual representation, the Wasserstein-1
distance between the empirical and generated transition distributions is

\begin{align}
W_1
\left(
P_{\mathrm{data}},
P_G
\right)
=
\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}_1}
\Bigg\{&
\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathrm{data}}}
\left[
C_{\psi}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t+h},
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t;
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{t,t+h}
\right)
\right]
\nonumber\\
&-
\mathbb{E}_{P_G}
\left[
C_{\psi}
\left(
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+h},
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t;
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{t,t+h}
\right)
\right]
\Bigg\},
\label{eq:ch3:wasserstein_distance}
\end{align}

where $\mathcal{C}_1$ denotes the class of 1-Lipschitz functions.
$P_{\mathrm{data}}$ denotes the empirical distribution of realized
current-to-future IVS transitions, while $P_G$ denotes the transition
distribution induced by sampling the observed current information from the
data, drawing $\mathbf{z}_t\sim P_z$, and generating
$\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+h}$ through $G_{\theta}$.


\paragraph*{Real and generated Critic scores.}
For a minibatch containing $B$ observations, the Critic assigns the following
scores to the realized and generated transitions:

\begin{align}
s^{\mathrm{real}}_b
&=
C_{\psi}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{b,t+h},
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{b,t};
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{b,t,t+h}
\right),
\label{eq:ch3:critic_real_score}\\
s^{\mathrm{fake}}_b
&=
C_{\psi}
\left(
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{b,t+h},
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{b,t};
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{b,t,t+h}
\right),
\qquad
b=1,\ldots,B.
\label{eq:ch3:critic_fake_score}
\end{align}

During the Critic update, $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{b,t+h}$ is treated as a detached Generator output, so the update changes only the Critic parameters $\psi$. The minibatch
estimates of the mean realized and generated scores are

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:critic_mean_scores}
\overline{s}_{\mathrm{real}}
=
\frac{1}{B}
\sum_{b=1}^{B}
s^{\mathrm{real}}_b,
\qquad
\overline{s}_{\mathrm{fake}}
=
\frac{1}{B}
\sum_{b=1}^{B}
s^{\mathrm{fake}}_b.
\end{equation}

Using a minimization convention, the empirical Wasserstein component of the Critic loss is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:critic_wasserstein_component}
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{W}}
=
\overline{s}_{\mathrm{fake}}
-
\overline{s}_{\mathrm{real}}
=
\frac{1}{B}
\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\left(
s^{\mathrm{fake}}_b
-
s^{\mathrm{real}}_b
\right).
\end{equation}

Minimizing $\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{W}}$ therefore decreases the
average generated-transition score and increases the average
realized-transition score.


\paragraph*{Gradient penalty.}
The Wasserstein formulation requires the Critic to satisfy a 1-Lipschitz
constraint. In this chapter, we further added a regularized ($\lambda_{\mathrm{GP}}$) gradient penalty term ($\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{GP}}$). The gradient penalty (GP) encourages the Critic to satisfy the 1-Lipschitz constraint by keeping its gradient norm close to one. This prevents the Critic from becoming excessively sensitive to small changes in the volatility surface and provides smoother gradients for the Generator, thereby improving WGAN training stability. Related gradient-penalty formulations have been studied by \cite{petzka2018regularization,mescheder2018training} and applied to convolutional generation, structured-data generation, and financial-market simulation \cite{karras2018progressive,xu2019modeling,han2022simulating}.

Following \citet{gulrajani2017improved}, the gradient-penalty coefficient was fixed at \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GP}}=10\) as an empirical regularization setting. For each minibatch observation, an interpolated future surface is constructed as:


\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:critic_interpolation}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{b,t+h}
=
\alpha_b
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{b,t+h}
+
\left(
1-\alpha_b
\right)
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{b,t+h},
\qquad
\alpha_b
\sim
\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1).
\end{equation}

The gradient of the Critic score with respect to this interpolated candidate
surface is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:critic_input_gradient}
\mathbf{g}_b
=
\nabla_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{b,t+h}}
C_{\psi}
\left(
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{b,t+h},
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{b,t};
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{b,t,t+h}
\right).
\end{equation}

The minibatch gradient penalty is then calculated as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:gradient_penalty}
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{GP}}
=
\frac{1}{B}
\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\left(
\left\|
\mathbf{g}_b
\right\|_2
-
1
\right)^2.
\end{equation}

The derivative is taken only with respect to the interpolated future surface.
The current IVS and joint-support mask are held fixed. Because the
interpolated surface is joint-support-masked before entering the Critic,
unsupported grid cells have zero influence on the Critic score and its input
gradient. The gradient penalty regularizes the Critic along sampled
interpolation paths; it does not by itself establish global Lipschitz
continuity over every possible input.


\paragraph*{Critic loss.}
The complete minibatch Critic loss combines the empirical Wasserstein
component and the gradient penalty:

\begin{align}
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{C}
&=
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{W}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{GP}}
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{GP}}
\nonumber\\
&=
\overline{s}_{\mathrm{fake}}
-
\overline{s}_{\mathrm{real}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{GP}}
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{GP}},
\label{eq:ch3:wgan_loss}
\end{align}

where $\lambda_{\mathrm{GP}}=10$ in this research. Equivalently, the
population-level loss is

\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{C}
={}&
\mathbb{E}_{P_G}
\left[
C_{\psi}
\left(
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{t+h},
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t;
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{t,t+h}
\right)
\right]
\nonumber\\
&-
\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathrm{data}}}
\left[
C_{\psi}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t+h},
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t;
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{t,t+h}
\right)
\right]
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{GP}}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GP}}.
\end{align}

The three terms have distinct roles:

\begin{itemize}
    \item
    $\overline{s}_{\mathrm{fake}}$ is minimized, encouraging lower Critic
    scores for generated transitions during the Critic update;
    \item
    $-\overline{s}_{\mathrm{real}}$ is minimized by increasing the scores
    assigned to realized transitions;
    \item
    $\lambda_{\mathrm{GP}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{GP}}$ penalizes
    deviations of the sampled input-gradient norm from one.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Generator adversarial loss.}
During the Generator update, the Critic parameters $\psi$ are held fixed and
the generated surface is no longer detached. The Generator is trained to
increase the Critic score assigned to its generated transitions. The
minibatch adversarial loss is therefore

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:generator_adversarial_loss}
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{adv}}
=
-
\frac{1}{B}
\sum_{b=1}^{B}
C_{\psi}
\left(
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{b,t+h},
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{b,t};
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{b,t,t+h}
\right)
=
-
\overline{s}_{\mathrm{fake}}.
\end{equation}

Because of the negative sign, minimizing
$\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{adv}}$ increases the Critic scores assigned to
generated transitions. This encourages the Generator to produce future IVSs
whose transitions from the current surface resemble empirically observed
market transitions.

Furthermore, adding the non-arbitrage panelty, the complete Generator loss is:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:generator_objective_summary}
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{G}
=
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{adv}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{rec}}
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{masked\,MAE}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{cal}}
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{cal}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{bfly}}
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{bfly}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{smooth}}
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{smooth}},
\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\mathrm{rec}}=10,
\qquad
\lambda_{\mathrm{cal}}=2,
\qquad
\lambda_{\mathrm{bfly}}=2,
\qquad
\lambda_{\mathrm{smooth}}=0.1.
\end{equation}

All terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch3:generator_objective_summary} are minibatch
averages, and the coefficient on the adversarial component is normalised to
one. The masked reconstruction term provides pointwise supervision over jointly
supported grid cells. The calendar-spread and butterfly-arbitrage terms are
the discrete penalties defined in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ch3:calendar_loss}
and~\eqref{eq:ch3:butterfly_loss}, respectively, while the smoothness term
regularizes local variation in the generated IVS. Consequently, the fitted
model is a hybrid supervised--adversarial forecaster rather than a pure
adversarial WGAN.




Finally, a well-trained generator should reproduce the distribution of the subsequent IVS while preserving its principal distributional and geometric characteristics, including its overall level, term structure, skew, curvature, and local dependence across the moneyness--maturity grid. The critic encourages the generated surfaces to remain consistent with the empirical characteristics of observed IVSs, while the generator learns a stochastic mapping from the information available at time $t$ to the distribution of the subsequent volatility surface.

\section{The Dataset}\label{ch3:sec:data}

In this section, we describe the datasets utilized in our research. The dataset comprises two primary components: (1) numerical data, including historical futures and options prices, and (2) textual data, consisting of relevant news articles. Our analysis focuses on the period from 17th March, 2022 to 27th July 2023, a time characterized by heightened economic uncertainty. During this interval, the Federal Reserve announced ten consecutive increases in the Federal Funds Rate (FFR), raising it from 0.50\% to 5.50\%. As the FFR serves as the benchmark rate for the U.S. economy, the Federal Reserve employs open market operations, such as the purchase and sale of government bonds, to guide the effective rate toward its target. These policy changes had a significant impact on the interest rate market. Accordingly, our research concentrates on this volatile period to investigate the effects of government announcements on the government bond option market dynamics.




\subsection*{Futures and Options Dataset}\label{sec: ch3: dataset: option}
We use the U.S. 10-Year Treasury Note (T-Note) future contracts and the future option contracts. Both the future and the option contracts are listed in Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME). All the contracts can be traded continuously except a one-hour halt from 4PM to 5Pm Central Time (CT). Hence, the contract can be regarded as trading for all the time allowing traders to reactions to economic events at almost anytime.


For the future contract, each contract has a face value of \$100,000 with a maturity of one-year. The contract is rolling, if one contract is expired, a new one-year contract will be opened automatically. There are 4 quarterly contracts listed with maturity months: March, June, September, and December. The contract is expired at the 7th business day before the end of maturity month. The contract is quoted on points with a basis of 100. Noticeably, the contract is American-style which allows the owner to exercise the right anytime before the maturity. 


The contracts are represented by 4 characters. For instance, ``TYM4\footnote{Refinitiv and Bloomberg use ``TY'' as the identifier for the U.S. 10-Year Treasury Note future contract, CME use ``ZN'' for the contract.}'' is the listed contract, where ``TY'' indicates that the underlying asset is the U.S. 10-year Treasury note, ``M'' denotes the expiration month (June),  and ``4'' signifies the last digit of the expiration year (2024).


The option contract is derived from one future contract but has maturities for each month. It is expired at the last business day of the maturity month. The option contract is American style which means traders can exercise the contract anytime before the maturity. Each option contract is identified by 6 to 8 characters.

For instance, ``TY11225T3'' is the listed contract name. ``TY'' indicates that the underlying asset is the 10-year T-note future contract. ``11225'' represents the strike price of the contract, which is 112.25 points. ``T'' serves as the indicator of the contract maturity. Also it distinguishes between call and put options, different types of options have different sets of indicators for the contract maturity month. The example contract's maturity month is August. The final digital, ``3'', means the contract's maturity year is 2023.

We extracted the limit order book data from the Refinitiv DataScope, a database vendor received data directly from the CME. Compared with the market order which is execute directly, although the order may not be executed, the limit order allows traders to submit specific prices about the asset based on their understanding about the market. Therefore, it can provide insights about their expectations about the asset.

As mentioned previously, the US 10-Year T-Note future contract is the most liquid government bond future contracts in the market. The data size is extremely large. But we utilize the accessability of Refinitiv DataScope and extracted the future and option contract data by developing a Python SDK. The chapter on the multithreaded Refinitiv DataScope SDK offers the engineering details about how we extract and process the data.


Finally, we extracted the tick data for both the futures and options contracts covering the period from January 1, 2022, to December 31, 2023. 


We utilize a 5-minute time interval ($h=5$) to construct the Implied Volatility Surface (IVS) in our main test. Since the option contracts are quoted in terms of price, we employ the Black Model (\cite{black76}, Eq.\ref{eq:ch3:black76}), which is the extended model for Black–Scholes model (\cite{BlackScholes:1973}) when the underlying asset is future contract ($F_t$), to invert the prices and compute the corresponding implied volatilities (IVs).

The raw-price inversion uses the actual UTC trade timestamp \(t\) and the contract's UTC expiration timestamp \(T\), including the intraday fraction:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:pricing_clock}
\tau^{\mathrm{act}}(t,T)
=\frac{\operatorname{seconds}(T-t)}{365\times86{,}400}.
\end{equation}

For the pricing equations below, write \(\tau=\tau^{\mathrm{act}}(t,T)\). Both the Black variance \(\sigma^2\tau\) and the discount factor \(e^{-r\tau}\) use this ACT/365 fraction. Consequently, the inverted IV is annualised on an actual-calendar-time basis. The business-day maturity \(q\) used to index and interpolate surfaces is a separate coordinate.


\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:black76}
    \begin{aligned}
        C_t^{\mathrm{bl}}(F_t,K,r,T,\sigma)
        &=e^{-r\tau}
          \left[F_t\Phi(d_1)-K\Phi(d_2)\right],\\
        P_t^{\mathrm{bl}}(F_t,K,r,T,\sigma)
        &=e^{-r\tau}
          \left[K\Phi(-d_2)-F_t\Phi(-d_1)\right].
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation*}
    \begin{split}
        d_1 &= \frac{\ln\left( \frac{F_t}{K} \right) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\tau}{\sigma \sqrt{\tau}} \\
        d_2 &= d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{\tau}
    \end{split}
\end{equation*}


where \(C_t^{\mathrm{bl}}(\cdot)\) and \(P_t^{\mathrm{bl}}(\cdot)\) are the
theoretical call and put values, \(T\) is the expiration time, \(r\) is the
risk-free rate, \(\sigma\) is implied volatility, and \(\Phi(\cdot)\) is the
standard normal cumulative distribution function. Therefore, for a given
market price \(C_{i,t}^{\mathrm{mkt}}\) or \(P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{mkt}}\), implied
volatility \(\sigma_{i,t}^{*}\) is obtained by minimizing the squared pricing
error. For a call, this is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:mse}
    \sigma_{i,t}^{*}
    =
    \underset{\sigma>0}{\arg\min}\,
    \left[
    C_{i,t}^{\mathrm{mkt}}
    -
    C_t^{\mathrm{bl}}(F_t,K_i,r,T_i,\sigma)
    \right]^2 .
\end{equation}


The executable inversion uses Brent's bracketed method to solve the Black price residual. For comparison, the Newton--Raphson update for the same root would be

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:newton}
    \sigma_{n+1}
    =
    \sigma_n-\frac{f(\sigma_n)}{f'(\sigma_n)}
    =
    \sigma_n
    -
    \frac{
    C_t^{\mathrm{bl}}(F_t,K_i,r,T_i,\sigma_n)
    -
    C_{i,t}^{\mathrm{mkt}}
    }{
    \operatorname{Vega}(\sigma_n)
    } .
\end{equation}

where \(f(\sigma)=C_t^{\mathrm{bl}}(F_t,K_i,r,T_i,\sigma)
-C_{i,t}^{\mathrm{mkt}}\) is the price residual, whose square appears in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch3:mse}, and \(\operatorname{Vega}(\cdot)\) is the option's sensitivity to implied volatility:

\begin{equation*}
    \begin{split}
        Vega(\sigma)
        &= \frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma} \\
        &=  e^{-r\tau} F_t \sqrt{\tau} \cdot \phi(d_1)
    \end{split}
\end{equation*}

Here, $\phi(\cdot)$ denotes the standard normal probability density function (PDF). The $Vega$ calculation is the same for both the call option and the put option.

\subsection*{Market--News Pair Construction}
\label{subsec:ch3:market_news_pairs}

The market--news dataset is constructed through two deliberately separated procedures. 

Inspired by the high-frequency price-discovery literature (\cite{andersen2007realtime, bradley2014analysts, grossklussmann2011machines} ), we first construct the full set of potential five-minute IVS pairs independently of the news data. Each potential pair links a current IVS constructed from the interval $[t-5,t)$ to a target IVS constructed from $[t,t+5)$. Both surfaces must belong to the same CME trading day, neither construction window may cross a market closure, and the two surfaces must share at least one actual maturity. 

Second, we match textual information to these potential IVS pairs using a forward-only alignment rule. Each article is assigned to the earliest eligible IVS-pair origin at or after its recorded availability time ($t$).  When multiple articles correspond to the same IVS pair, duplicate articles are removed and their embeddings are pooled to form one pair-level textual representation. 

Separately, each eligible IVS pair is evaluated for unusually large realized changes in the ATM implied-volatility level, local smile skew, and rolling ATM-jump skewness. Pairs exceeding the pre-specified thresholds are assigned frozen market-jump labels before textual information or model forecast errors are examined. These labels are subsequently joined to the news-aligned prediction panel by market-origin time and used in RQ4 to test whether the incremental forecasting value of correctly matched text is greater during retrospectively identified market-jump periods than during comparable ordinary-market periods.


 

\paragraph*{Five-minute market transitions}
Let $t$ denote the origin of a market transition and let $h=5$ minutes. The
current IVS is constructed from option observations in the half-open interval
$[t-5,t)$, while the target IVS is constructed from observations in
$[t,t+5)$. Thus, a five-minute market transition is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:five_minute_market_pair}
p_t
=
\left(
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t},
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t+h}
\right),
\qquad
h=5\text{ minutes}.
\end{equation}

Both intervals must lie within the same CME trading day without crossing a market closure, and
the two snapshots must share at least one actual option maturity. The frozen
market index contains 34,480 indexed five-minute transition records. Eight
transitions cross a trading-day boundary and a further 22 have no common actual
maturity; these observations remain visible in the audit data but are excluded
from the evaluable universe. This leaves a predeclared set
$\mathcal O_{\mathrm{mkt}}$ of 34,450 eligible market-transition origins
against which news can subsequently be aligned.


\paragraph*{Market jump detection.}
Market changes are measured before the news join. For endpoint
$u\in\{t,t+h\}$, nominal strike $K$, and matched futures price $F_u$, define
moneyness and log-moneyness by $q_u(K)=K/F_u$ and
$k_u(K)=\log q_u(K)$. Within each common actual maturity, the current and
target slices must refer to the same underlying futures contract and share the
nominal strike used in the comparison. The common approximate-ATM strike
$K^{*}_{t,T}$ is selected by minimizing
$\max_{u\in\{t,t+h\}}\lvert k_u(K)\rvert$ across the common strikes. The
corresponding five-minute ATM-IV change is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:atm_iv_jump}
\Delta\sigma^{\mathrm{ATM}}_{t,T}
=
\sigma_{t+h,T}\!\left(K^{*}_{t,T}\right)
-
\sigma_{t,T}\!\left(K^{*}_{t,T}\right).
\end{equation}

Let
$d^{\mathrm{ATM}}_{t,T}=\max_{u\in\{t,t+h\}}
\lvert K^{*}_{t,T}/F_u-1\rvert$. Approximate-ATM support receives its best
available class: A if $d^{\mathrm{ATM}}_{t,T}\leq1\%$, B if
$1\%<d^{\mathrm{ATM}}_{t,T}\leq2\%$, C if
$2\%<d^{\mathrm{ATM}}_{t,T}\leq5\%$, and unusable otherwise. Thus,
log-moneyness determines which common strike is nearest ATM, whereas the
percentage-moneyness distance determines its reported quality class. This
observed common-strike rule avoids treating an interpolated value at $K/F=1$
as an observed ATM quote.

We next measure changes in the local shape of the volatility smile. Among
nominal strikes observed at both endpoints and lying within 5\% of the forward
price at both times, let $K_L$ and $K_R$ be the nearest common strikes that
remain on the left and right of ATM, respectively, at both endpoints. When
both endpoints have this two-sided support and each log-moneyness span is at
least 0.005, the local smile skew and its five-minute change are defined as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:local_smile_skew}
\begin{aligned}
S_{u,T}
&=
\frac{
\sigma_{u,T}(K_R)-\sigma_{u,T}(K_L)
}{
k_u(K_R)-k_u(K_L)
},
\qquad u\in\{t,t+h\},\\
\Delta S_{t,T}
&=
S_{t+h,T}-S_{t,T}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Here, local smile skew is an IV slope across log-moneyness and should not be
confused with the statistical skewness of a time series. For this smile metric,
quality classes A and B additionally require at least three common near-ATM
strikes, while C requires at least two, every class remains subject to the
two-sided-support and minimum-span conditions above.

As a separate distributional diagnostic, we calculate rolling
bias-corrected Fisher--Pearson sample skewness from A- or B-quality signed
five-minute ATM-IV changes. Let $g=(T,c)$ identify a
\texttt{maturity\_date $\times$ underlying\_contract}
group. For a clock-time window of $w$ minutes, define

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:rolling_jump_skew_index}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal I^{(w)}_{g,t}
&=
\left\{
j:
t-w\leq t_j\leq t,\;
Q^{\mathrm{ATM}}_j\in\{A,B\},\;
t_j\text{ is in the same continuous run as }t
\right\},\\
n^{(w)}_{g,t}
&=
\left\lvert\mathcal I^{(w)}_{g,t}\right\rvert .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Writing $\overline{\Delta\sigma}^{(w)}_{g,t}$ and $s^{(w)}_{g,t}$ for the sample mean and sample standard deviation over this set, the adjusted coefficient, when the required count is met and $s^{(w)}_{g,t}>0$, is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:rolling_jump_skew_estimator}
\widehat\gamma^{(w)}_{g,t}
=
\frac{n^{(w)}_{g,t}}
{\bigl(n^{(w)}_{g,t}-1\bigr)\bigl(n^{(w)}_{g,t}-2\bigr)}
\sum_{j\in\mathcal I^{(w)}_{g,t}}
\left(
\frac{
\Delta\sigma^{\mathrm{ATM}}_{t_j,T}
-\overline{\Delta\sigma}^{(w)}_{g,t}
}{s^{(w)}_{g,t}}
\right)^3 .
\end{equation}

The 30-, 60-, and 120-minute windows require at least 15, 30, and 60 observations, respectively, and a gap longer than five minutes starts a new
continuous run. Only

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:rolling_jump_skew_change}
\Delta\widehat\gamma^{(60)}_{g,t}
=
\widehat\gamma^{(60)}_{g,t}
-
\widehat\gamma^{(60)}_{g,t^-}
\end{equation}

enters the anomaly screen, where $t^-$ is the preceding time in the same run at which the 60-minute estimate is finite. Because the underlying five-minute changes overlap, this time-series statistic is treated as an auxiliary signal
and cannot independently receive a high or primary label.

The three screened quantities are therefore the ATM-IV change, the local smile-skew change, and the change in rolling ATM-jump skewness. Each signed quantity is scored separately within metric, the UTC calendar year of its origin, and the origin business-day maturity buckets 1--5, 6--21, 22--63, and 64--107 days. For an observation $x_i$, the primary robust standardization is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:robust_jump_score}
z^{\mathrm{rob}}_i
=
\frac{x_i-\operatorname{median}(x)}
{1.4826\,\operatorname{MAD}(x)},
\end{equation}

with IQR- and sample-standard-deviation fallbacks when the MAD is zero. Its absolute empirical percentile is the within-stratum percentile rank of
$\lvert x_i\rvert$, $p_i=\lvert\mathcal G_i\rvert^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal G_i} \mathbf{1}\{\lvert x_j\rvert\leq\lvert x_i\rvert\}$, where $\mathcal G_i$ denotes observation $i$'s scoring stratum. A stratum must contain at least 30 observations. A non-rolling metric is classified as \emph{high} when it has A-quality support, lies at or above the
99.9th percentile, and satisfies $\lvert z^{\mathrm{rob}}_i\rvert\geq6$; it is
classified as \emph{primary} when it has A- or B-quality support, lies at or
above the 99.5th percentile, and satisfies
$\lvert z^{\mathrm{rob}}_i\rvert\geq5$. A metric is classified as \emph{broad}
when it has A-, B-, or C-quality support and either lies at or above the 99th
percentile or satisfies $\lvert z^{\mathrm{rob}}_i\rvert\geq4$. The rolling
skewness diagnostic can generate only a broad label.


Figure~\ref{fig:ch3:rolling_skew_filter_result} shows the frozen full-sample
time-series output from this rolling-skewness screen.

maturity--metric observation receives a broad, primary, or high anomaly label.
This market-first screen yields 1,244 candidate transitions with mutually
exclusive pair-level highest tiers: 31 high, 156 primary, and 1,057 broad.
Within each trading day, candidate intervals are merged transitively whenever
the next origin occurs no later than five minutes after the current episode
end, producing 461 market episodes. Only after the rankings and episode
membership are frozen are Factiva reports and official macroeconomic

are $[s-5,s)$ for pre-event context, $[s,e)$ for the episode, and $[e,e+15)$
for subsequent reporting, where $s$ and $e$ denote the episode start and end.



\paragraph*{Five-minute market-news pair construction.}
All 14,900 Factiva source rows enter the alignment audit. Their stored publication dates and times (in minutes) are interpreted in the \texttt{Europe/London} time zone and then converted to UTC, including daylight saving-time
transitions. 

Let the availability minute of article $a$ be $\tau_a$. For an article released while the CME market is open, the primary alignment selects the earliest origin in the same CME trading day, without crossing a market closure, satisfying

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:news_market_origin}
t_a
=
\min\left\{
t\in\mathcal O_{\mathrm{mkt}}:
\begin{aligned}
&0\leq t-\tau_a\leq5\text{ minutes},\\
&\operatorname{tradingday}(t)=\operatorname{tradingday}(\tau_a),\\
&[\tau_a,t]\text{ contains no market closure}
\end{aligned}
\right\}.
\end{equation}

Here, $\operatorname{tradingday}(x)$ denotes the archived CME trading-day identifier associated with timestamp $x$.

The current and target surfaces paired for 5-minute window with article $a$ are therefore obtained from

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:news_market_windows}
\mathcal W^{\mathrm{current}}_a=[t_a-5,t_a),
\qquad
\mathcal W^{\mathrm{target}}_a=[t_a,t_a+5).
\end{equation}

If the selected contract subsequently fails surface construction, the article is excluded rather than rematched to a later transition. 


Finally, the primary five-minute alignment matches 1,628 open-market source rows. Of these, 992 match a market origin at the publication minute. In this exact match pairs, the current window is entirely pre-news and the target window is post-news. 




\paragraph*{Target construction and the maturity-clock distinction.}

The matched observations are converted into current and target
raw-data-derived gridded IVSs on the same exact-TTM $16\times16$ grid. The moneyness axis contains 16 evenly spaced nodes over $K/F\in[0.97,1.03]$, and the time-to-maturity axis, measured in business days, is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:exact_ttm_nodes}
\mathcal T
=
\{1,2,3,6,7,8,9,10,14,15,17,21,26,30,35,38\}.
\end{equation}

The current and target grids retain the scale of the raw ACT/365 IVs. Within each business-day maturity and strike bucket, the construction uses a volume-weighted median and interpolates a total-variance proxy on the fixed grid. The business-day maturity coordinate used for this interpolation does not reannualise the IVs or replace the actual expiry fraction used in the raw-price inversion. Appendix~\ref{app:ch3_target_interpolation} gives the full interpolation rule, maturity-clock distinction, and reproduction audit.


After that, The pipeline deliberately preserves three distinct analytical layers. 

First of all, our main dataset \texttt{gan\_input}  contains every open-market source row whose selected transition has usable current and target surfaces. At five minutes this view contains 1,610 source rows representing 1,283 unique market transitions.


% Second, \texttt{gan\_input\_atm\_ab}, a subsect used for auditing and maturity-level ATM/skew diagnostics, required  at least one actual maturity with an A- or B-quality common-strike ATM measure. It contains 1,311 source rows and 1,051 pairs at five minutes. 


Then, the model loader constructs the raw joint-support mask in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch3:critic_joint_support_mask} directly from the surface-usable view. A cell is retained only when both raw endpoint surfaces support it without strike clamping or maturity extrapolation, and a pair must retain at least one such cell, which lead to 1,026 pair-level observations left. 




Moreover, multiple source rows can map to the same market transition. These rows are retained to preserve article-level lineage. If pair \(p\) is associated with \(n_p\) source rows, each row is assigned weight \(w_{p,i}=1/n_p\), so that

\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^{n_p} w_{p,i}=1.
\end{equation}

Consequently, every market transition contributes the same total weight, irrespective of the number of associated articles, preventing news-intensive periods from being over-represented. Before model estimation, duplicate
articles are removed, their text embeddings are pooled, and the retained transition is collapsed into one pair-level observation with unit weight.

Finally, their 1,024-dimensional LP embeddings are averaged and $L_2$-normalised as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch3:pair_lp_embedding}. The loader then collapses each retained transition to one pair-level observation with unit
weight.

\subsection*{Four rolling temporal folds.}
To increase the prediction stability, we employed 4 rolling dataset folders in this research.  The four folds follow a rolling-origin evaluation with an expanding training window. At each forecast origin, the model is estimated exclusively from past observations, the immediately subsequent quarter is used for validation and checkpoint selection, and the following quarter is reserved for out-of-sample testing. This construction prevents future information from entering model fitting or data-dependent preprocessing. Evaluating the model at multiple forecast origins also reduces dependence on the market conditions of a single test period and reveals whether forecasting performance remains stable across changing regimes \citep{tashman2000outofsample}. The design is consistent with evidence that temporally ordered out-of-sample procedures are preferable when serial dependence and non-stationarity may render unrestricted random cross-validation misleading \citep{bergmeir2012use,cerqueira2020evaluating,hewamalage2023forecast}.


More specifically, let $t(p)$ denote that origin for pair $p$. For rolling fold $r$, boundaries $a_r<b_r<c_r$ define

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:rolling_fold_partition}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal D^{\mathrm{train}}_{w,r}
&=
\left\{p\in\mathcal D_w:t(p)<a_r\right\},\\
\mathcal D^{\mathrm{validation}}_{w,r}
&=
\left\{p\in\mathcal D_w:a_r\leq t(p)<b_r\right\},\\
\mathcal D^{\mathrm{test}}_{w,r}
&=
\left\{p\in\mathcal D_w:b_r\leq t(p)<c_r\right\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where \(p\) indexes an eligible news--market pair, \(\mathcal D_w\) denotes the
complete set of pairs constructed under alignment window \(w\), and \(t(p)\)
is the market-origin timestamp of pair \(p\). The index \(r\) identifies the
rolling fold, while \(a_r\), \(b_r\), and \(c_r\) denote, respectively, the
start of the validation period, the start of the test period, and the end of
the test period. Consequently, the training set contains all pairs before
\(a_r\), whereas the validation and test sets occupy the non-overlapping
half-open intervals \([a_r,b_r)\) and \([b_r,c_r)\). The half-open boundaries
ensure that each pair is assigned to exactly one partition within a fold.


Thus, the training window expands through time, while validation and test are
adjacent non-overlapping calendar quarters.

At each roll, the validation quarter from the preceding fold is incorporated
into the expanding training sample, the preceding test quarter becomes the new
validation period, and the subsequent quarter becomes the new test period.
Within a given fold, the training, validation, and test partitions are mutually
pair-disjoint. Reuse across folds is nevertheless intentional: as the forecast
origin advances, a pair evaluated in an earlier fold may enter the training
sample of a later fold, consistent with the expanding-window rolling-origin
design.


The same calendar boundaries \(a_r\), \(b_r\), and \(c_r\) are applied to every
alignment-window population \(\mathcal D_w\), although the number of eligible
pairs may vary with \(w\). Partition membership is determined exclusively by
the pair-origin timestamp \(t(p)\) and is independent of the text
representation. To prevent information leakage, all representation-specific
transformations requiring estimation, including the BoW vocabulary, text
centering, principal-component removal, and sentiment scaling, are fitted
using only the training partition of the corresponding fold and are then held
fixed when applied to its validation and test partitions.

\subsection*{Evaluation Design}
The primary evaluation uses all four rolling test folds, 2023Q1--2023Q4. The folds contain 110, 112, 135, and 143 eligible market--news pairs, respectively, giving 500 unique pairs and 148 trading days in total. Each following model or training condition is estimated using ten seeds in each fold, giving 40 seed--fold cells. Forecasts use the same exact-TTM $16\times16$ grid, raw joint-support mask, and common bank of
64 Monte Carlo noise draws. Quarterly results describe variation within this four-fold evaluation. 

First of all, we defined a persistence benchmark. The persistence benchmark is a no-change forecast rather than an estimated model. For each forecast origin \(t\), the current implied-volatility surface \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t}\) is constructed from observations in the interval \([t-5,t)\), while the realized target surface \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t+5}\) is constructed from observations in \([t,t+5)\). The persistence forecast simply carries the current surface forward:
\[
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\,\mathrm{pers}}_{t+5}
=
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t}.
\]
Evaluation is restricted to grid cells supported by both surfaces. Let
\[
\mathbf{M}^{\cap}_{t,t+5}
=
\mathbf{M}_{t}\odot\mathbf{M}_{t+5}
\]
denote this joint-support mask. The persistence error for surface pair \(i\) is therefore
\[
\operatorname{MAE}^{\mathrm{pers}}_{i}
=
\frac{
\sum_{j,k} M^{\cap}_{i,j,k}
\left|
\sigma_{i,t,j,k}-\sigma_{i,t+5,j,k}
\right|
}{
\sum_{j,k} M^{\cap}_{i,j,k}
}.
\]


The same pair-level masked loss is used for every model, using each seed's 64-draw mean forecast. The ten seed-level losses are then averaged. Let $e_{a,s,f,i}$ be that loss for model $a$, seed $s$, fold $f$, and eligible pair $i$, and let $N_f$ be the number of pairs in the fold. The original-sample MAE point estimands are defined before any bootstrap resampling as


\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3:observed_mae_weighting}
\widehat{\operatorname{MAE}}_{a,s,f}
=
\frac{1}{N_f}
\sum_{i=1}^{N_f}e_{a,s,f,i},
\qquad
\widehat{\operatorname{MAE}}_{a,f}
=
\frac{1}{S}
\sum_{s=1}^{S}
\widehat{\operatorname{MAE}}_{a,s,f},
\qquad
\widehat{\operatorname{MAE}}_a
=
\frac{1}{4}
\sum_{f=1}^{4}
\widehat{\operatorname{MAE}}_{a,f}.
\end{equation}




Model performance relative to persistence is summarized by
\[
\operatorname{Gap}
=
\operatorname{MAE}_{\mathrm{model}}
-
\operatorname{MAE}_{\mathrm{pers}},
\]
where a negative value indicates that the model outperforms the persistence forecast. Equivalently, the percentage improvement may be reported as
\[
\operatorname{Improvement}(\%)
=
100\left(
1-
\frac{\operatorname{MAE}_{\mathrm{model}}}
{\operatorname{MAE}_{\mathrm{pers}}}
\right),
\]
for which a positive value indicates superior model performance.  


Paired inference is based on a separately defined proportional-loss contrast.
Its original-sample point estimate assigns equal weight to each seed--fold
cell:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3:observed_log_ratio_weighting}
\widehat{\theta}_{a:b}
=
\frac{1}{4S}
\sum_{f=1}^{4}
\sum_{s=1}^{S}
\log\!\left(
\frac{
\widehat{\operatorname{MAE}}_{a,s,f}
}{
\widehat{\operatorname{MAE}}_{b,s,f}
}
\right),
\qquad
G_{a:b}
=
100\left[1-\exp\!\left(\widehat{\theta}_{a:b}\right)\right].
\end{equation}

Here, a negative value of \(\widehat{\theta}_{a:b}\) indicates that model
\(a\) has a lower MAE than model \(b\), while a positive value of
\(G_{a:b}\) represents the corresponding geometric percentage reduction in
MAE achieved by model \(a\).

The finally bootstrap analyses use 10,000 draws from a common
shared-market-panel procedure. Each bootstrap draw is constructed in three stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item the ten model seeds are sampled with replacement;
    \item for an analysis covering all four rolling folds, four fold occurrences are sampled with replacement, whereas a quarter-specific analysis holds the corresponding fold fixed.
    \item within each sampled fold occurrence, the observed number of trading days is sampled with replacement, and all eligible market pairs belonging to each selected trading day are retained.
\end{enumerate}

Within a given draw, the same fold and trading-day sampling schedule is reused
for every sampled seed and every focal or reference forecasting condition.
This common schedule preserves the paired structure of the comparisons: all
forecasting conditions are evaluated on the same resampled market observations,
so their differences are not confounded by independently drawn market
compositions.

Let \(\widehat{\theta}^{*(b)}\) denote the comparison statistic recomputed in bootstrap draw \(b\). Bootstrap standard errors are calculated as the standard deviation of the 10,000 draw-level statistics, while the reported 95\% confidence interval is given by their empirical 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. When a negative value favours the focal model, the one-sided bootstrap sign-tail probability is

\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{sign},-}
=
\frac{
1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\mathbf{1}\!\left\{
\widehat{\theta}^{*(b)}\geq0
\right\}
}{
B+1
},
\qquad
B=10{,}000.
\end{equation}

The \(+1\) correction prevents an estimated probability of exactly zero.
Where several comparisons form a pre-specified family, the resulting
one-sided probabilities are adjusted using Holm's procedure. Statistical
significance is displayed using

\[
{}^{*}p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<0.10,
\qquad
{}^{**}p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<0.05,
\qquad
{}^{***}p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<0.01.
\]

The parenthesized value reported beneath selected point estimates is

\[
\frac{
\widehat{\theta}
}{
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{boot}}
},
\]

where \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{boot}}\) is the standard deviation of the corresponding bootstrap distribution.

\subsection*{Main Results: FiLM-CNN versus Pure-CNN Baseline}
\label{subsec:ch3:film_pure_cnn_baseline}

The first research question evaluates the proposed forecasting system against
a surface-only neural benchmark. The Text baseline model (denoted FiLM-CNN), uses a
c32 convolutional U-Net Generator with skip connections and an identity
residual output. Its 1,024-dimensional matched LP representation is compressed
to a 128-dimensional text vector and conditions six intermediate convolutional
stages through FiLM. The Non-text baseline (denoted Pure-CNN) retains the same four
surface-derived inputs, c32 U-Net layout, skip connections, and residual-output
construction, but removes the text encoder and all FiLM transformations.  This
brief comparison emphasizes the experimental distinction; the layer-by-layer
definitions are given in the model section above. Both systems use the same
Critic. The FiLM-CNN and Pure-CNN Generators contain 827,745 and 416,353 trainable parameters,
respectively, while the common Critic contains 729,157 parameters.


\paragraph*{Training procedure.}
Both models were trained directly from random initialization rather than
from a shared parent checkpoint. Each of the four rolling folds uses ten
independently seeded fits per model, with its own preceding training and
validation partitions as listed in the temporal-split table above. There are
therefore 40 fits per model, not ten Q4-only fits. Training uses a batch size of 16, five Critic updates per Generator update, no learning-rate warm-up, and at most 240 epochs.  The
WGAN-GP, masked reconstruction, no-arbitrage, and smoothness objectives are
identical across the two baselines.


The Pure-CNN Generator and common Critic start at a learning rate of $5\times10^{-7}$. In FiLM-CNN, the convolutional backbone uses the same rate, whereas the text encoder and FiLM projections use $2.5\times10^{-6}$ and $2.5\times10^{-5}$, respectively. A plateau scheduler reduces each learning rate towards its prescribed floor. After a minimum of 30 epochs, training stops when the validation criterion has not improved for 20 epochs (early-stop), otherwise it continues to the 240-epoch limit. The checkpoint with the lowest validation hybrid score is retained. In every fold, validation uses 16 Monte Carlo draws and test prediction uses the common bank of 64 draws.

\paragraph*{Conclusion.}
Relative to persistence, FiLM-CNN reduces its mean-across-draw MAE by 0.0658\% in
2023Q1, 0.2556\% in 2023Q2, and 0.3540\% in 2023Q4, but increases it by
0.1369\% in 2023Q3. Pure-CNN follows the same temporal pattern, with
respective improvements of 0.0457\%, 0.1771\%, $-0.0871\%$, and 0.3372\%. For both
models, the improvement over persistence reaches the 1\% level after
Holm adjustment in 2023Q2 and only the 10\% level in 2023Q4
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0570$), but neither level in 2023Q1 or 2023Q3. Forecast
performance is therefore period-dependent rather than uniformly better than
persistence.

The direct architecture comparison is favourable to FiLM-CNN in three of the
four test folds. The fold-specific geometric gains are 0.0212\%, 0.0795\%,
$-0.0423\%$, and 0.0168\% in chronological order. After adjustment across
the four quarterly comparisons, none reaches the 10\% significance
level, the smallest adjusted sign-tail probability is 0.1524 in 2023Q2.
Giving the four folds equal weight within each draw, the overall
mean-across-draw MAEs are 0.0020450122 for FiLM-CNN and 0.0020455455 for
Pure-CNN, compared with persistence at 0.0020481318. These draw means are
descriptive and are distinct from the following original-sample contrast.
The overall geometric MAE reduction of 0.0188\%. This result indicated that FiLM-CNN performs slightly better in three of the four quarters, but none of the quarterly paired comparisons provides statistically significant evidence that it outperforms Pure-CNN across the rolling evaluation.


Overall, FiLM-CNN has better average
performance than Pure-CNN, but the ordering reverses in 2023Q3 and the overall
paired comparison is not significant. The subsequent representation and
common-parent experiments examine the contribution of textual information
more directly.



\subsubsection*{Text Representation Effectiveness}
\label{subsec:ch3:rq2_text_representation}

The second step examines whether the form in which news is represented affects IVS
forecasting while holding the predictive architecture fixed. Bag-of-words
(BoW) is a transparent frequency-based benchmark, it represents a document by
the occurrence counts of vocabulary items and therefore discards word order.
Such representations can preserve informative terms and short phrases, but
they cannot directly distinguish contextual meanings or polysemous uses of the
same term. In financial applications, BoW-style textual features have been
used to predict short-horizon stock prices from breaking news
\citep{schumaker2009textual}. More broadly, \citet{manela2017news} use
newspaper n-gram frequencies to construct a news-implied volatility measure,
showing that simple lexical frequencies can contain economically relevant
information about market uncertainty.



Sentiment analysis instead compresses a document into a small number of
economically interpretable scores. Media pessimism has been associated with
market activity and subsequent returns \citep{tetlock2007giving}, while the
tone of firm-specific news has also been linked to prices and earnings
\citep{tetlock2008more}. Financial text requires particular care because
general-purpose word lists can misclassify domain-specific language
\citep{loughran2011liability}. Measures of unusual news have additionally
been shown to forecast indicators of market stress \citep{glasserman2019unusual}. Furthermore, the sentiment score generated by Large Language Model (LLM) has been used in VIX prediction \citep{sun2026zeroshot}.




Therefore, becides the text embedding representation used in our main model, we compared the prediction performance with these two different representations, BoW and sentiment score.


\paragraph*{Representation construction.}
For every market--news pair $p$, articles are first deduplicated by article
identifier. Let $\mathcal{A}_p$ denote the resulting set of unique articles.
The matched LP reference averages the 1,024-dimensional article embeddings and
then applies $\ell_2$ normalization:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:rq2_lp_pair}
\mathbf{l}_p
=
\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_p|}
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_p}\mathbf{l}_a}
{\left\|\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_p|}
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_p}\mathbf{l}_a\right\|_2}.
\end{equation}


For BoW, inspired by \cite{manela2017news}, we construct a fold-specific unigram–bigram count vector, which mainly captures the relative composition of words and short phrases. Pair-level counts are transformed using \(\log(1+c)\) and subsequently L2-normalized. The restriction to the 1,024 most frequent training-partition terms is an experiment-specific dimensionality constraint.

If $N(v_k,a)$ is the count of vocabulary item $v_k$ in article $a$, pair-level counts and the normalized BoW vector are

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:rq2_bow_pair}
\begin{aligned}
c_{p,k} &= \sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_p}N(v_k,a),\\
\mathbf{b}_p
&=\frac{\log(1+\mathbf{c}_p)}
{\left\|\log(1+\mathbf{c}_p)\right\|_2}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}


For sentiment score, the input is constructed from the OPENAI's \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini}
model.


Following \citet{sun2026zeroshot} (also see \citet{baker2016measuring, jurado2015measuring, dungey2018testing,baele2020flights,tetlock2007giving}), we use an adapted zero-shot prompt extract the sentiment score from each article across three dimensions: macroeconomic uncertainty, institutional action, and risk-off intensity. Each score will be in $[0,1]$ Then, the scores are averaged within the pair and standardized using the mean $\mu_{f,j}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{f,j}$ estimated from training pairs in fold $f$:


\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:rq2_sentiment_pair}
\begin{aligned}
\bar{\mathbf{s}}_p
&=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_p|}
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_p}\mathbf{s}_a,\\
z_{p,j}
&=\frac{\bar{s}_{p,j}-\mu_{f,j}}{\sigma_{f,j}},
\qquad j\in\{1,2,3\},\\
\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{sent}}_p
&=(z_{p,1},z_{p,2},z_{p,3},0,\ldots,0)
\in\mathbb{R}^{1024}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

When a training-partition sentiment standard deviation is zero, the
implementation replaces the corresponding denominator by one. Likewise, an
LP or BoW vector with zero norm remains the zero vector rather than being
divided by zero. The BoW vocabulary and the sentiment means and standard
deviations are fitted separately on each fold's training partition to
preventing information from later partitions from entering text preprocessing.

\paragraph*{Common architecture and training.}
All three representations enter the same c32 FiLM-CNN. Its Generator maps the
1,024-dimensional text input through a $1024\rightarrow256\rightarrow128$
encoder and uses the resulting vector at six FiLM sites in the convolutional
U-Net. The Generator has 827,745 trainable parameters. Each representation
condition also uses the same 729,157-parameter Critic, in which the text contribution is
neutralized. Although sentiment has only three non-zero input dimensions,
zero-padding it to 1,024 dimensions keeps the network architecture and
parameter count identical across the LP, BoW, and sentiment conditions.

The four-fold analysis comprises
$3\times4\times10=120$ direct fits: three text representations, four rolling
folds, and ten seeds. Every fit starts directly from random initialization;
no parent checkpoint or continuation stage is used. Within each
fold--seed cell, the three representation conditions share the same initial
Generator and Critic states. Across F1--F4, the training partitions contain
382, 526, 636, and 748 pairs, while the adjacent validation partitions contain
144, 110, 112, and 135 pairs, respectively. The corresponding frozen test
partitions contain 110, 112, 135, and 143 pairs, as reported in
Table~\ref{tab:ch3:rolling_fold_construction}. All three representation
conditions use the same temporal split within a fold, batch size of 16, five Critic updates per Generator
update, objective functions, noise banks, and validation checkpoint-selection
rule.

The initial learning rates are $5\times10^{-7}$ for the convolutional
backbone, $2.5\times10^{-6}$ for the text encoder,
$2.5\times10^{-5}$ for the FiLM projections, and $5\times10^{-7}$ for the
Critic. Training lasts at most 240 epochs, with a minimum of 30 epochs and
patience of 20 epochs. Checkpoint selection uses 16 Monte Carlo draws on the
fold-specific validation data. After checkpoint selection is frozen, test
prediction uses a common bank of 64 Monte Carlo draws within each fold.

Table~\ref{tab:ch3:rq2_training_diagnostics} reports the checkpoint-selection
diagnostics across all four folds. Aggregated over the 40 fits per
representation, the mean best epochs are 88.0 for the matched text embedding,
94.1 for BoW, and 87.4 for sentiment. Each representation early-stops in 32
fits and reaches the 240-epoch limit in eight. The fold-specific results,
however, reveal substantial temporal heterogeneity. For example, BoW's mean
best epoch declines from 183.1 in 2023Q1 to 28.6 in 2023Q4, while sentiment's
mean declines from 145.9 to 10.8 but rises temporarily to 128.0 in 2023Q3.
These diagnostics describe optimization and checkpoint selection; they do not
by themselves establish that one representation learns economically useful
information faster than another.

\paragraph*{Interpretation and conclusion.}
Panels A--C of Table~\ref{tab:ch3:rq2_direct_results} show substantial
variation across the four evaluation periods. The estimated geometric gains
relative to persistence are positive for all three representations in
2023Q1, ranging from 0.0592\% to 0.0752\%, although none of the corresponding
paired contrasts meets a conventional Holm-adjusted significance threshold.
In 2023Q2, the gains range from 0.2034\% to 0.3149\%, and all three
model-versus-persistence contrasts meet the 1\% Holm-adjusted threshold. In
2023Q3, by contrast, all three log-MAE ratios are positive and their geometric
gains are negative, indicating that all three learned forecasts perform worse
than persistence. The gains become positive again in 2023Q4, ranging from
0.1952\% to 0.3863\%, but the corresponding contrasts meet only the 10\%
threshold. Forecasting performance relative to persistence is therefore
period dependent rather than uniformly favourable.

Panel D shows that the relative ordering of BoW and the matched text embedding
also changes across quarters. The LP-versus-BoW log-MAE ratio is positive in
2023Q1, 2023Q2, and 2023Q4, corresponding to geometric gains of
$-0.0056\%$, $-0.0558\%$, and $-0.0302\%$, respectively, and therefore
marginally favouring BoW in those periods. In 2023Q3, however,
$\widehat{\theta}=-0.005555$, corresponding to a 0.5540\% geometric gain for
matched LP. The descriptive standard-error ratio is
$\widehat{\theta}/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{boot}}=-1.54$, and this
contrast meets the 5\% threshold within that quarter's Holm-2 family
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0434$). Thus, the only quarter favouring matched LP over
BoW is also the only statistically significant quarterly contrast. The
evidence does not establish temporally stable superiority of matched LP over
BoW.

The LP-versus-sentiment comparison is directionally more consistent.
Its estimated log-MAE ratio is negative in every quarter, with geometric
gains of 0.0103\%, 0.0561\%, 0.4884\%, and 0.1613\% from 2023Q1 through
2023Q4. Nevertheless, only the 2023Q3 contrast meets the 5\% within-quarter
Holm-adjusted threshold:
$\widehat{\theta}=-0.004896$,
$\widehat{\theta}/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{boot}}=-1.76$, and
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0358$. The remaining quarterly contrasts are not
statistically significant; in particular, the adjusted probability in
2023Q4 is 0.1100. Moreover, because all three learned representations perform
worse than persistence in 2023Q3, the significant representation contrasts
in that quarter establish only a relative ranking among the fitted pipelines.
They do not show that matched LP improves forecasting relative to the
persistence benchmark.

For completeness, the frozen four-fold aggregate comparisons provide
complementary summaries but are not reported in Panel D, which presents
quarter-specific inference. In the separate aggregate comparison with BoW,
the four-fold equal-cell mean log-MAE ratio is
$\widehat{\theta}=-0.001160$, corresponding to a geometric gain of 0.1159\%.
The associated descriptive standard-error ratio is
$\widehat{\theta}/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{boot}}=-0.72$, with
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.3234$. Although the aggregate point estimate favours
matched LP in all ten seed-level aggregates, its direction favours LP in only
one of the four folds. The comparatively large 2023Q3 difference therefore
outweighs the smaller opposite-signed differences in the other quarters. This
aggregate result should not be interpreted as stable superiority of matched
LP over BoW.

The separate four-fold LP-versus-sentiment comparison gives
$\widehat{\theta}=-0.001794$, equivalent to a geometric gain of 0.1792\%,
with
$\widehat{\theta}/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{boot}}=-1.41$ and
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0612$. Its direction favours matched LP in all four
folds and in all ten seed-level aggregates. This directional pattern is more
stable than the comparison with BoW, but the aggregate reaches only the 10\%
level and not the 5\% Holm-adjusted threshold; its 95\% percentile interval
also includes zero. The result is therefore suggestive rather than a verified
representation advantage. The seed counts describe stability across repeated
initialisations evaluated on the same market observations, not ten independent
market replications. These two aggregate contrasts form a separate overall
RQ2 Holm-2 family from the four quarter-specific Holm-2 families reported in
Panel D.

One possible, but not identified, explanation is that compressing each article
set into three sentiment dimensions omits distinctions useful to this
forecasting pipeline. The fold-specific BoW representation retains unigram
and bigram occurrence information but does not encode broader context, whereas
the matched LP embedding may preserve richer contextual variation. However,
the representations also differ in dimensionality, sparsity, scaling,
construction, external pretrained-model information, and optimisation
behaviour. The experiment therefore cannot attribute the observed ranking
causally to contextual information or to any other individual representation
property. Overall, RQ2 provides directionally consistent but non-confirmatory
aggregate evidence in favour of matched LP relative to sentiment, while its
advantage over BoW is concentrated in 2023Q3 and is not stable across market
periods.

\subsubsection*{Incremental Text Value}
\label{subsec:ch3:rq3_incremental_text_value}

\paragraph*{Four-fold common-parent design.}
The direct-training comparisons in RQ1 and RQ2 cannot isolate the incremental
value of text because they differ in both model architecture and optimization
path. RQ3 therefore evaluates whether textual conditioning provides an
incremental forecasting gain under a common-parent design across the same four
rolling folds and ten seeds used in RQ1 and RQ2.

This design operationalises the information-set comparison in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch3:forecast_information_sets}: every branch starts from the same
surface-only forecasting rule, while the text input varies under the specified
controls. For observations with a post-publication current window, the parent
already observes any news response captured in that window. A subsequent
textual gain would therefore concern predictive information remaining after
that observed response, rather than the size of the initial market reaction.

For each of the 40 seed--fold cells, a Pure-CNN model is first trained without
text using only the corresponding expanding training partition. Its
validation-best checkpoint is selected using the immediately preceding
validation quarter and is then frozen. The training, validation, and test
partitions are those reported in
Table~\ref{tab:ch3:rolling_fold_construction}. Consequently, neither the
corresponding test fold nor any later observation contributes to parent
training or checkpoint selection.

The shared \texttt{surface\_encoder}, \texttt{bottleneck\_conv},
\texttt{decoder\_convs}, and \texttt{residual\_head} parameters are copied
exactly from the Pure-CNN parent into a FiLM-CNN Generator. A new
$1024\rightarrow256\rightarrow128$ text encoder and six zero-initialized FiLM
projections are then added. The Critic is also copied tensor for tensor
without changing its parameters. At the graft point, matched, zero, and
shuffled LP inputs all reproduce the Pure-CNN parent output exactly. Across
all graft manifests, the recorded maximum absolute difference is zero, which
satisfies the required tolerance of at most $10^{-7}$.

Optimizer and scheduler states are not inherited from the parent. Both Adam
optimizers are initialized with empty state, and both learning-rate schedulers
are initialized from their fresh states. A deterministic branch
RNG and DataLoader state is initialized for each seed--fold cell and shared
across its continuation branches. The continuation branches therefore use the same
batch ordering and training-noise sequences without inheriting the parent's
Adam moments.

Each frozen parent defines six equal-protocol continuation branches:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item additional Pure-CNN training without text;
    \item FiLM-CNN with a zero text vector;
    \item FiLM-CNN with correctly matched LP embeddings;
    \item FiLM-CNN with deterministically shuffled LP embeddings;
    \item FiLM-CNN with BoW inputs; and
    \item FiLM-CNN with sentiment inputs.
\end{enumerate}

The five FiLM branches begin from the same FiLM graft state, while the
Pure-CNN continuation begins from the corresponding frozen Pure-CNN parent
state. Within each seed--fold cell, all six branches share the prescribed
batch ordering and training-noise sequence. The convolutional backbone, text
encoder, FiLM projections, and Critic have initial learning rates of
$5\times10^{-7}$, $2.5\times10^{-6}$, $2.5\times10^{-5}$, and
$5\times10^{-7}$, respectively. Both the parent and continuation stages permit
at most 240 training epochs, with a minimum training duration of 30 epochs and
an early-stopping patience of 20 epochs. The 30-epoch minimum governs when
training may stop; it does not prevent an earlier checkpoint from being
selected as validation-best. Each continuation branch applies the same
validation checkpoint-selection rule independently. BoW and sentiment are
retained as descriptive representation diagnostics, whereas the controls
frozen for incremental-text-value assessment are the
equal-budget zero-text and shuffled-LP branches.

The complete four-fold experiment contains

\[
4\ \text{folds}\times10\ \text{seeds}=40
\]

Pure-CNN parents and

\[
40\ \text{parents}\times6\ \text{continuations}=240
\]

continuation jobs, giving 280 completed training jobs in total. It also
contains 280 standard frozen-test prediction cells and 80 additional
same-checkpoint intervention cells in which the matched-LP checkpoint is
evaluated using zero or independently shuffled text.

The four frozen test panels contain, respectively, 110 pairs from 34 
trading days in 2023Q1, 112 pairs from 36 trading days in 2023Q2, 135 pairs from
33 trading days in 2023Q3, and 143 pairs from 45 trading days in 2023Q4. Their union
contains 500 unique market--news pairs from 148 trading days. These same
frozen panels are evaluated under each of the ten seeds; the seeds do not
generate different test observations. Validation prediction uses 16 Monte
Carlo draws, while frozen-test prediction uses a common bank of 64 draws
within each paired comparison. 

Table~\ref{tab:ch3:rq3_training_diagnostics} summarizes checkpoint-selection
behaviour across the four folds. The matched-LP continuation selects its
validation-best checkpoint at a mean epoch of 38.9 and a median epoch of 7.0,
whereas the corresponding Pure-CNN continuation selects at a mean epoch of
142.4 and a median epoch of 239.5. This difference describes optimization and
checkpoint-selection behaviour, but it does not by itself establish that text
accelerates useful learning. The controlled validation-MAE trajectories and
the frozen-test comparisons against the equal-budget zero-text and
shuffled-text branches provide the relevant evidence.

\paragraph*{Frozen branch inference.}

incorrectly paired LP text.  Because all branches begin from the same grafted

separate text-specific gains from those arising from the shared architecture
or additional optimization alone.

For reference condition $r$, the point estimand is the equal-cell mean
log-MAE ratio across ten seeds and four rolling test folds:

\begin{equation}
\widehat{\theta}_{r}
=
\frac{1}{40}
\sum_{s=1}^{10}
\sum_{f=1}^{4}
\log\left(
\frac{\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{matched},s,f}}

\subsubsection*{Greater Text Value during Events}
\label{subsec:ch3:rq4_conditional_text_value}

We further examine whether correctly matched LP text is relatively more useful
during scheduled-announcement or unusually large market-movement periods than
during comparable ordinary-market periods.





The primary matched-control analysis uses model predictions generated and frozen before the event--control sets are constructed. Matching is performed without reference to model predictions or forecast errors, and the same frozen sets are applied to every model and seed. The supplementary transfer analysis applies the previously selected checkpoints described in Section~\ref{subsec:ch3:film_pure_cnn_baseline} to all eligible special-time pairs. In both layers, event labels are used only for evaluation and do not enter model training, checkpoint selection, or hyperparameter tuning.



We use two complementary layers of evidence. The primary layer conducts a matched event--ordinary-control analysis to test whether correctly matched LP provides greater incremental forecasting value during event periods than during comparable ordinary periods. The supplementary layer evaluates the frozen FiLM-CNN and Pure-CNN models on every eligible special-time pair, including those without a successful ordinary-control match. It therefore broadens the assessment of special-time forecasting performance but does not test whether text is relatively more valuable during event periods.


Let $e_{a,s,f,p}$ denote the pair-level IVS MAE for forecasting condition $a$,
seed $s$, fold $f$, and market pair $p$.  Here, $a$ indexes matched LP,
no-text continuation, and shuffled LP.  For either reference condition
$r$---no-text continuation or shuffled LP---define the matched-LP text
advantage as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:rq4_text_advantage}
A^{(r)}_{s,f,p}
=
e_{r,s,f,p}-e_{\mathrm{LP},s,f,p}.
\end{equation}

A positive value means that matched LP has lower forecast error than the
reference model.  For scheduled window $w$, let $\mathcal M_{w,f}$ denote the
frozen matched sets in fold $f$, where set $m$ contains event pair $p^E_m$ and
ordinary control $p^C_m$, and let $\mathcal C_w$ denote the observed seed--fold
cells.  The reported conditional text-value estimand is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:rq4_conditional_increment}
\widehat{\Delta}^{(r)}_w
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal C_w|}
\sum_{(s,f)\in\mathcal C_w}
\frac{1}{|\mathcal M_{w,f}|}
\sum_{m\in\mathcal M_{w,f}}
\left(
A^{(r)}_{s,f,p^E_m}-A^{(r)}_{s,f,p^C_m}
\right).
\end{equation}


The estimator first averages within each seed--fold cell and then gives the
observed cells equal weight.  Positive $\widehat{\Delta}^{(r)}_w$ indicates
that matched LP is relatively more valuable during the event period, while a
negative value indicates that its advantage is smaller than for the matched
ordinary control.  This is a difference-in-differences contrast in absolute
MAE units.

frozen calendar contains 168 releases during 2022--2023 across the eight
families reported in Table~\ref{tab:ch3:scheduled_event_calendar}.  It is
constructed from the issuing institutions' official release schedules,
retains only observations explicitly classified as scheduled, assigns each
release a unique event identifier, and converts its local release time to UTC

$H_0:\Delta^{(r)}_w\leq0$ against $H_1:\Delta^{(r)}_w>0$.
This is not a two-sided test of any regime difference: failure to support an
increase neither establishes equal text value across regimes nor rules out a
decrease during event periods.

The primary analysis is considered sufficiently covered only when it contains
at least 30 matched event--control sets, 20 selected releases, and 10
event-side trading days.  Under the pre-specified evidence rule, support for
greater event-period text value additionally requires a positive point estimate, a
95\% confidence interval with a lower bound above zero, a Holm-adjusted
$p$-value below 0.05, and a favourable direction in at least seven of the ten
seeds and three of the four rolling folds.  These conditions jointly guard
against conclusions driven by a small event sample, a single model
initialisation, or one test period.  Table~\ref{tab:ch3:rq4_conditional_results}
reports the scheduled-announcement matched-control estimates.

\paragraph*{Fold-pooled frozen-checkpoint transfer design.}
The primary matched-control analysis includes only event pairs for which an
eligible ordinary control can be identified.  Although this restriction
improves comparability between event and ordinary periods, it necessarily
excludes special-time observations that cannot be matched.  We therefore add
a supplementary frozen-checkpoint transfer diagnostic that evaluates the
direct FiLM-CNN and Pure-CNN systems on every eligible special-time pair
in the frozen 30-minute, raw-joint event universe.  This diagnostic does not
require an ordinary control and consequently provides broader coverage of
forecasting behaviour during special-time periods.  Its purpose is
descriptive: it neither repairs the coverage failure of the matched-control
analysis nor isolates a causal or text-specific event effect.


For each rolling checkpoint fold, the event observations assigned to the
training, validation, and test calendar periods are concatenated into a single
inference panel.  The original \texttt{source\_split} field is retained so
that the provenance of every observation remains auditable and the test-only
subset can subsequently be recovered without generating new predictions.  A
pair is admitted when its forecast origin lies in the inclusive scheduled
release window $[0,+30]$ minutes or when its exact UTC origin matches a frozen
all-tier market-jump pair.  These two conditions define a set union rather
than mutually exclusive samples.  A pair satisfying both conditions appears
only once and is labelled \texttt{both}; the remaining observations are
labelled \texttt{scheduled\_only} or \texttt{jump\_only}.  Eligibility is
determined independently of whether the pair received an ordinary control in
the primary analysis.  The news-alignment tolerance remains 30 minutes, while
the forecasting target remains the IVS exactly five minutes after the current
surface.  Let $\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{RQ4}}_f$ denote the resulting frozen
special-time panel for checkpoint fold $f$, with
$N_f=|\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{RQ4}}_f|$.

As reported in Table~\ref{tab:ch3:rq4_fold_pooled_combined}, the panels for F1--F4 contain 102, 128, 154, and 185 fold-routed pairs, respectively.


The diagnostic loads the FiLM-CNN checkpoints and the
Pure-CNN checkpoints from RQ1.  Each checkpoint is used
once to predict its complete fold panel.  For every pair, the reported surface
is the arithmetic mean of 64 stable Gaussian-noise draws.  FiLM-CNN and
Pure-CNN use the same noise keys for each seed--fold--pair cell.  If
$M_{f,p,i}$ is the current/target raw-support intersection at grid cell $i$,
the pair-level error is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:rq4_fold_pooled_pair_mae}
e_{m,s,f,p}
=
\frac{
\sum_{i=1}^{256}M_{f,p,i}
\left|\widehat{Y}_{m,s,f,p,i}-Y_{f,p,i}\right|
}{
\sum_{i=1}^{256}M_{f,p,i}
},
\end{equation}

where $m\in\{\mathrm{FiLM},\mathrm{PureCNN}\}$ and
$\widehat{Y}_{m,s,f,p}$ is the 64-draw mean surface.  Each mask must contain at
least one supported cell, and $M_{f,p,i}$ is binary.  The within-cell pooled
MAE is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch3:rq4_fold_pooled_cell_mae}
E^{\mathrm{all}}_{m,s,f}
=
\frac{1}{N_f}
\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_f^{\mathrm{RQ4}}}
e_{m,s,f,p}.
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:ch3:rq4_fold_pooled_pair_mae} fixes the pair-level unit of
analysis, while Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch3:rq4_fold_pooled_cell_mae} prevents the larger
later-fold panels from receiving more weight in the primary combined summary.
The primary combined summary gives each of the 40 seed--fold cells equal
weight.  A second descriptive summary gives each of the 569 fold-routed pairs
equal weight after balanced replication over the ten seeds.

\paragraph*{Conclusion.}
The scheduled-announcement analysis satisfies its frozen primary coverage
requirements, allowing the planned matched-control comparisons to be
estimated.  Nevertheless, neither the no-text nor the shuffled-LP contrast
provides statistically significant or directionally consistent evidence that
matched LP becomes relatively more valuable around scheduled releases.  The
robustness and descriptive announcement windows lead to the same general
conclusion.  The supplementary fold-pooled diagnostic expands
coverage to all eligible special-time candidates and produces a small
descriptive advantage for the frozen FiLM-CNN system over Pure-CNN.  However,
this direct-model comparison jointly changes access to text, conditioning
architecture, and model capacity.  Its ordering also reverses in the
30-minute-only alignment stratum, while the test-only FiLM/Pure confidence
interval includes one and the favourable direction appears in only two of the
four folds.  The supplementary result therefore neither resolves the missing-
control problem in the market-jump analysis nor establishes greater
conditional value from matched text.

Taken together, the available event evidence does not verify greater
forecasting value from text during event periods.  The increase-only tests
do not establish equal value across regimes or rule out a negative
event-minus-ordinary increment.  The event--ordinary-control comparisons remain retrospective and
exploratory, while the source-split-restricted transfer analysis provides
rolling out-of-sample evidence only relative to the corresponding
checkpoints' fitting and selection.  Because the test periods had already been
examined during model development, this evidence is not equivalent to an
untouched confirmatory holdout.

\subsection*{Robustness Tests}

This section examines whether the main findings are sensitive to three modelling choices: the Generator conditioning architecture, Generator capacity, and the news--market alignment tolerance. Unless stated otherwise, these experiments retain the same data, five-minute forecasting horizon, Critic, checkpoint-selection procedure, and evaluation process used in the main analysis.


\providecommand{\rqthreerobustcell}[3]{%
\shortstack{\ensuremath{#1^{{#2}}}\\[-0.2ex]
{\scriptsize\ensuremath{(#3)}}}}


\subsubsection*{Generator-Architecture Robustness}


Our first robustness test examines whether the main findings depend on the
Generator's conditioning architecture. Our main model's Generator is a
convolutional encoder--decoder with U-Net-style skip connections
\citep{ronneberger2015unet}. It incorporates the LP news representation through
feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM), which applies text-conditioned,
channel-wise affine transformations---scaling and shifting---to intermediate
feature maps \citep{perez2018film}. We compare it with three matched-capacity
alternatives: a cross-attention U-Net, a Transformer-token Generator, and a
style-modulated (StyleMod) U-Net.


First, the cross-attention U-Net draws on the additive encoder--decoder
attention mechanism of \citet{bahdanau2015neural} and implements cross-attention
using the multi-head scaled dot-product formulation introduced by
\citet{vaswani2017attention}. In cross-attention, the queries and the key--value
pairs are derived from different representations. In our implementation,
spatial volatility-surface features provide the queries, whereas the common
$1024\rightarrow256\rightarrow128$ LP encoder is projected into four
64-dimensional virtual text tokens that provide the keys and values.
Four-head cross-attention is applied at the $4\times4$ bottleneck and the
$8\times8$ and $16\times16$ decoder stages. Each attention output enters
through a learned residual gate initialized at zero, so the convolutional path
is initially unchanged. The variant retains the U-Net encoder, decoder, and
skip connections but, unlike FiLM's spatially broadcast channel-wise
transformation, allows individual moneyness--maturity cells to place different
weights on the text tokens.


Second, the Transformer-token Generator adapts the Transformer encoder
architecture of \citet{vaswani2017attention} to the volatility-surface grid and
removes the convolutional U-Net entirely. Each of the 256 cells on the
$16\times16$ grid becomes a token constructed from masked current IV, the
support mask, normalized moneyness, and normalized log time to maturity. These
cell tokens are projected to 128 dimensions and augmented with learned row and
column positional embeddings. One encoded LP token and one token encoding the
32-dimensional Gaussian noise vector are prepended, giving a sequence of 258
tokens. Three Transformer encoder layers, each using four-head self-attention,
then jointly update the surface, text, and noise representations. A shared
zero-initialized head maps the 256 updated surface tokens back to a residual
surface. This design replaces the local receptive-field bias of the CNN with
global cell-to-cell and cell-to-conditioning interactions.

Finally, the StyleMod U-Net adapts StyleGAN2's modulated-convolution and
per-output-channel weight-demodulation formulation
\citep[Section~2.2]{karras2020analyzing}; it does not reproduce the complete
StyleGAN2 synthesis network. The common LP encoder is projected to a shared
256-dimensional style vector. Separate layer-specific affine maps convert this
vector into one modulation coefficient per input channel for six $3\times3$
convolutions---three encoder layers, the bottleneck, and two decoder layers.
For each sample, the convolutional weights are first scaled along the
input-channel dimension and then normalized separately for each output channel
by the corresponding $L_2$ norm, with a small constant added for numerical
stability. Unlike StyleGAN2's layer-wise stochastic noise injection, our
Gaussian noise vector is broadcast and concatenated only at the bottleneck,
while the U-Net skip connections continue to carry local surface information.
Whereas FiLM scales and shifts activations after an ordinary convolution,
StyleMod makes the effective convolutional filters text- and sample-dependent.
The modulation coefficients are parameterized around the identity: the
text-dependent residual is initialized at zero, so the initial conditioning
effect is neutral.


The five-minute market--news dataset is evaluated using three seeds
(42, 202, and 404) over all four rolling folds. The common test panel contains
110, 112, 135, and 143 pairs in 2023Q1--2023Q4, respectively, giving 500
unique pairs and 12 seed--fold cells per model. The alternatives change the
Generator while retaining the data, Critic, and evaluation contracts.
Deterministic parameter matching produces 824,644, 834,561, and 829,635
Generator parameters for cross-attention, Transformer-token, and StyleMod,
respectively, compared with 827,745 for FiLM-CNN.

Reported MAE retains the original pooled-pair estimand used for this robustness
study.  For model $k$, all 1,500 seed--pair losses are pooled, so the three
seeds receive equal weight and folds receive weight in proportion to their
110, 112, 135, and 143 test pairs.  Writing this pooled mean as
$\widehat E^{\mathrm{pool}}_k$, the primary architecture contrast is

\[
\widehat{\theta}_{k}
=
\log\!\left(
\frac{\widehat E^{\mathrm{pool}}_k}
     {\widehat E^{\mathrm{pool}}_{\mathrm{FiLM\text{-}CNN}}}
\right),
\]

where a negative value and a positive geometric gain
$100[1-\exp(\widehat{\theta}_k)]$ favour the alternative.  Under this pooled
estimand, the geometric gain equals the arithmetic percentage reduction
computed from the two unrounded displayed observed MAEs.

\subsubsection*{News--Market Alignment-Window Robustness}

In this section, we further examine the model's sensitivity to different
news-to-market alignment tolerances. More specifically, we vary the maximum permitted delay between the time at which a news item becomes available and the origin of its first eligible market pair. Let $\tau_i$ denote the availability time of news item $i$.
For an alignment tolerance $\Delta\in\{5,10,15,20,30\}$ minutes, we retain the first eligible market origin $t_i$ satisfying
\[
0 \leq t_i-\tau_i \leq \Delta.
\]
The forecasting horizon is nevertheless fixed at five minutes for every
value of $\Delta$: the model uses the current surface over
$[t_i-5,t_i)$ to predict the subsequent surface over $[t_i,t_i+5)$.

Increasing $\Delta$ changes the number and temporal composition of the
eligible news--market pairs, including how much post-publication trading can
enter the observed current surface. Every comparison still asks whether text
helps predict the next five-minute IVS transition given that surface.
The five cumulative tolerances are 5, 10, 15, 20, and 30 minutes. After applying the same strict raw joint-support rule, their complete data universes contain 1,026, 1,196, 1,294, 1,371, and 1,475 pairs, respectively.


\[
\mathcal D_{5}
\subset
\mathcal D_{10}
\subset
\mathcal D_{15}
\subset
\mathcal D_{20}
\subset
\mathcal D_{30}.
\]

At every tolerance, the study evaluated c32 FiLM-CNN with matched LP and c32
Pure-CNN without text for 3 seeds over all four rolling folds. The 24 five-minute baseline cells were reused read-only, and the remaining 96
cells were trained from random initialization under the same validation-only
checkpoint-selection and Monte Carlo prediction contracts. The FiLM
conditioning learning rate was fixed at $2.5\times10^{-5}$. Terminal QA passed
for all 96 new training jobs, 324 evaluation units, and 46,044 pair-metric
rows.

one-sided comparisons do not establish a FiLM-CNN gain at any tolerance, nor
do they constitute equivalence tests between the two models.




\section{Conclusion}

This chapter asks whether correctly matched news text improves forecasts of
the next five-minute implied volatility surface (IVS) transition after the
current surface is observed. In the studied market and period, the evidence
does not establish a stable incremental forecasting gain from text. This
finding concerns conditional prediction of the observed surface; it does not
test whether news moves option prices or whether a text-based trading strategy
has economic value.

The analysis links Federal Reserve-related Dow Jones Newswires articles to
options on U.S. 10-Year Treasury Note futures. It evaluates changes between
current and future surfaces on a common $16\times16$ business-day grid, with
forecast error measured only where both observed surfaces have raw support.
Four expanding-window rolling folds keep model fitting and validation-based
checkpoint selection earlier than each test quarter. The main experiments
repeat fitting across ten seeds and use paired market observations, common
Monte Carlo noise, and shared-market resampling. This design distinguishes
tests of complete-system performance from tests of incremental value from
correctly matched text.

For RQ1, the directly trained FiLM-CNN has a $0.0188\%$ geometric MAE
advantage over the surface-only Pure-CNN across the four equally weighted
test folds, with a one-sided unadjusted $p=0.3200$. Its lower aggregate point
error is therefore not a statistically supported system-level advantage.
The ordering reverses in 2023Q3, and neither system improves uniformly on
persistence across the four quarters: both reach the 1\% Holm-adjusted level
in 2023Q2, whereas the 2023Q4 comparisons reach only the 10\% level
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0570$). The two systems also differ in architecture and
parameter count, so their direct comparison cannot isolate the value of
textual information.

RQ2 holds the FiLM-CNN architecture and training protocol fixed while varying
the text representation. Relative to sentiment scores, the LP embedding has
a $0.1792\%$ geometric MAE advantage ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0612$), with a
favourable direction in all four folds. Relative to bag-of-words features,
its advantage is $0.1159\%$ ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.3234$), driven mainly by
the 2023Q3 comparison. Neither four-fold aggregate contrast reaches the 5\%
Holm-adjusted criterion. Representation rankings under a fixed architecture
consequently do not establish that the fitted forecaster benefits from the correctly
matched content of an article.

RQ3 addresses that attribution more directly. Common-parent continuation
branches inherit the same convolutional state and use equal training budgets
and paired test observations. Matched LP has a $0.1245\%$ geometric MAE
advantage over the shuffled-LP branch ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.3174$), but its
advantage over the zero-text branch is only $0.0072\%$
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.4768$). Neither branch contrast is statistically
supported. When the matched-LP checkpoint and prediction noise are held
fixed, replacing its input with zero or incorrectly paired text likewise
does not increase aggregate original-sample MAE. These aggregate results do
not imply identical predictions for individual pairs or prove that the
benefit of text is zero. Under the chapter's evidence rule, beneficial
reliance on correctly matched LP text remains unverified.

RQ4 examines whether incremental text value rises around scheduled
announcements or unusually large market movements. The scheduled-announcement
comparison meets its coverage requirement with 30 matched sets, but neither
matched-control contrast supports a greater event-period benefit. The
market-jump comparison does not meet its prespecified matched-pair requirement
and cannot estimate that contrast. A supplementary comparison of the complete
FiLM-CNN and Pure-CNN systems also cannot separate text from their other
architectural differences. Its broader transfer pool includes observations
from training and validation periods; in the restricted rolling-test subset,
the FiLM-CNN point advantage is $0.0574\%$ and the two-sided $p$-value is
0.5987. The
event-conditional question therefore remains unresolved. The directional
tests do not establish equal text value across regimes or exclude a lower
event-period value.

The robustness analyses do not overturn this interpretation. None of the
three alternative conditioning architectures shows a statistically supported
improvement over FiLM-CNN, and their fixed-checkpoint interventions provide
no supported evidence of beneficial reliance on matched LP. In the separate
F4 (2023Q4) capacity exercise, c32 has the lowest observed three-seed MAE
among six widths for both FiLM-CNN (0.0016690756) and Pure-CNN
(0.0016692538). This descriptive result concerns one fold and does not
establish an optimal capacity across periods or repeat the RQ3 text-value
tests at alternative widths. Widening the news--market alignment tolerance
from five to 30 minutes increases own-panel test coverage from 500 to 721
pairs. On the fixed five-minute evaluation panel, the within-model tests do
not support a forecast-accuracy gain from wider training tolerances.
Own-panel MAEs use different pair universes across tolerances, so their
five-minute-normalized improvement columns are descriptive and cannot rank
the tolerances' predictive accuracy on a common population.

The current surface may already contain part of a news response when its
observation window overlaps post-publication trading. The weak incremental
text results are compatible with that explanation, but also with limitations
in text representation, learning, timing measurement, or the short forecast
horizon; the present tests cannot distinguish these mechanisms. The chapter's
methodological contribution is to make that uncertainty visible. Chronological
market--news lineage, common-support scoring, common-parent controls,
fixed-checkpoint replacements, and explicit event-coverage rules reduce the
risk of interpreting a small difference between forecasting systems as
verified incremental information from matched news text.

These conclusions are specific to one fixed-income option market, one
newswire, the 2022--2023 monetary-policy environment, a five-minute horizon,
and the cells supported on the study's IVS grid. Recorded publication times
proxy for availability, while retrospectively constructed embeddings do not
establish that the same text representations were available at each forecast
origin. The rolling test quarters are later than their respective fitting and
selection periods, but they were inspected during thesis development and are
not an untouched confirmatory holdout. Repeated seeds do not create new
market observations, and the trading-day bootstrap cannot remove all temporal
dependence or create additional regimes. The limited event sample further
constrains inference. Finally, the American exercise feature complicates a
European Black interpretation, and masked MAE of the mean generated surface
does not assess predictive-distribution calibration, option-pricing accuracy,
hedging outcomes, or trading returns net of costs.

Further research should use new forward periods, as-of text encoders and
message-level delivery records, additional option markets, and longer
forecast horizons. Larger prospectively specified event samples could test
whether matched text contributes differently across market conditions.
Distributional scoring, no-arbitrage assessment, and pricing and hedging
evaluation would test whether any future forecast improvement is
economically meaningful. Within the present design, stable incremental
predictive value from correctly matched text remains unverified.
